# The Person Who Signs Their Name

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** O. Careers, institutions, and the economics of expertise | **Candidate tags:** ethics, sociology-of-science

The name on a report may once have given us a reasonable indication of who performed much of the work of writing it. Increasingly, it can tell us something different: who has agreed to explain the report, answer for its use, and arrange for its correction if the conclusions prove mistaken.

It may still tell us something important. The person could be the one who has undertaken to answer questions about the report, correct it when a mistake is found, and explain why it was used to make a decision. Those obligations are different from the work of composing the document.

Machine-assisted production makes the distinction between preparing an account and accepting responsibility for it particularly consequential. A professional may have access to an extraordinary ability to generate analyses. The organization still needs to decide who can authorize their use and what that authorization means.

There is a danger in placing someone's name on a process they cannot understand or influence. A nominally responsible person may have too little time or information to carry out the obligations assigned to them. Requiring a signature does not resolve that problem.

A person reviewing a generated analysis may discover that the data needed to verify its central claim are held by another organization. Giving them responsibility for the analysis without arranging access to that evidence makes their role difficult to fulfill. A meaningful commitment requires conditions under which the signatory can investigate and, if necessary, decline to authorize the result.

A signature becomes meaningful when the institution gives the signatory a way to carry the obligations it represents. That includes access to the relevant evidence, time to examine it, and the authority to challenge the result. Assigning responsibility to a person who cannot exercise those powers gives an institution a name to point to without establishing that anyone can fulfill the commitment.
