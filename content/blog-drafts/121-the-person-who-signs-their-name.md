# The Person Who Signs Their Name

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** O. Careers, institutions, and the economics of expertise | **Candidate tags:** ethics, sociology-of-science

A report arrives with someone's name on it. The person may have written every sentence. They may have asked an assistant to prepare the document and then reviewed it. The name tells us less about the history of production than it might once have done.

It may still tell us something important. The person could be the one who has undertaken to answer questions about the report, correct it when a mistake is found, and explain why it was used to make a decision. Those obligations are different from the work of composing the document.

As agents become more capable, I expect the relation between producing an account and being responsible for it to become more explicit. A professional may have access to an extraordinary ability to generate analyses. The organization still needs to decide who can authorize their use and what that authorization means.

There is a danger in placing someone's name on a process they cannot understand or influence. A nominally responsible person may have too little time or information to carry out the obligations assigned to them. Requiring a signature does not resolve that problem.

I would like institutions to distinguish the responsibility someone accepts from the labor they personally perform. A useful arrangement should give that person the authority and resources needed to fulfill the responsibility. It should also make it possible to identify other parties whose decisions shape the result. A signature ought to tell us something about the commitments that follow, even when it cannot tell us who produced each part of the work.
