# The Form That Everyone Fills Out Wrong

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** K. Interaction, delegation, and responsibility | **Candidate tags:** computer-science, participatory-design, sociology-of-science

Suppose the people in a workplace repeatedly enter information in a field of a form that was designed for something else. It would be easy to prevent the incorrect entry. I would first want to know which part of their work depends on being able to make it. The error may be evidence of a mismatch between the official representation of the work and the work itself.

Perhaps the official categories do not describe the work very well. People might use one field to leave information that a colleague needs, because the form offers no other place for it. What looks like an error in the record can be part of a useful local arrangement.

This does not mean we should preserve every workaround. Informal conventions can be fragile, difficult for newcomers to learn, and dangerous when important information is interpreted incorrectly. But removing a workaround without understanding its purpose can make the actual work harder.

Imagine a form whose date field is used to indicate when someone needs a colleague's attention because the interface has no way to express urgency. Validating the date format would remove the unofficial signal. The useful change may be to represent urgency explicitly, provided the organization agrees about how that information should affect the work.

Agents that follow formal procedures consistently can expose this discrepancy. An agent can follow the official rules consistently. It may still encounter situations in which the people doing the work depend on conventions that the rules do not describe. A better system might help them make those conventions explicit, or give them a reason to develop a different practice.

Before making the form stricter, the designer could follow the information to the person who needs it. If the practice serves a legitimate purpose, a new field or a different procedure may support it more reliably. The discrepancy gives us a way to discover what an organizational system needs to represent, rather than merely an opportunity to improve compliance with its existing categories.
