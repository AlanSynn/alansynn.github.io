# Expertise as a Subscription

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** O. Careers, institutions, and the economics of expertise | **Candidate tags:** career-advice, computer-science, sociology-of-science

A person's productive capacity can change considerably when a service they use is updated, even if their own knowledge has remained the same. They may have learned an efficient way of working with an assistant and become dependent on capabilities that the next version no longer provides. What part of their expertise was attached to the service?

The question is especially consequential when we evaluate a person's ability to move to a different workplace.

Part of their ability belonged to the relationship they had established with a particular tool. Another part may have belonged to their own understanding of the problems they were solving. They might be able to transfer that understanding to another system. Some of the arrangements they had developed may be difficult to reproduce.

This is not entirely new. Professionals have long depended on specialized instruments, software, and institutions. The dependence can become broader when a single service participates in many of the intellectual activities that once distinguished an expert. A person's productive capability may change substantially without that person acquiring or losing knowledge in the ordinary sense.

The useful distinction is between knowledge a person can demonstrate in another environment and a workflow that depends on a service's particular behavior. A transition may preserve the domain knowledge, some of the artifacts, and little of the automation that connected them. Examining an actual transition would tell us more about dependence than simply counting how often someone used the service.

I find this an important consideration for thinking about careers. It is useful to understand which relationships with tools can be rebuilt and which are difficult to move. Someone who has learned to describe a problem, evaluate a result, and recognize a relevant constraint may have a different kind of continuity from someone who has learned a productive sequence of commands for a particular system.

As professional work comes to depend on more extensive computational assistance, the ability to work may increasingly be something a person has access to rather than something they can carry from one institution to another. Understanding that difference means examining which knowledge, artifacts, and workflows can survive a change in provider. Neither portability nor knowledge guarantees a livelihood, but both affect the terms on which someone can participate in the next project.
