# The Prototype That Has to Stay

**Status:** editorial draft / argument development | **Theme:** Graphics, fabrication, and making | **Candidate tags:** participatory-design, sociology-of-science, ethics

A research prototype acquires a different significance when someone begins to depend on it. The researchers may regard the prototype as a means of investigating an idea. The person using it may have incorporated it into work that will continue long after the study ends.

Suppose a small organization adopts a fabricated device developed during a research collaboration. The device performs a useful task. Its users gradually develop ways of organizing their work around it. When a part breaks a year later, they discover that the design is difficult to modify and that the people who understood it have moved on.

The original research may have produced useful knowledge. It has also created a practical dependency. The value of the artifact to the organization now depends on arrangements that were not necessarily part of the original experiment: access to the design, availability of replacement parts, knowledge of the mechanism, and someone with the ability to repair it.

This distinction matters when making a working prototype becomes inexpensive. A group can generate many objects and tools without establishing the conditions under which the people receiving them can continue to use them. An initial demonstration can make those conditions relatively easy to overlook because the original builders are still available to help.

Not every research prototype needs to become a maintained product. A clearly bounded exploratory study can give everyone good reasons to understand that an artifact will be withdrawn. The obligation changes when the researchers encourage reliance or make commitments that give the people involved a reason to expect continued access.

One way to make the obligation intelligible is to agree in advance on who will own the design files, obtain spare parts, and decide whether the device should remain in use. If continued support cannot be provided, a transition plan may be appropriate. These decisions should reflect the reliance the project has encouraged, rather than treating every experimental prototype as a commercial product.

A useful evaluation of a deployed prototype might therefore continue after the first demonstration. Can another person identify a problem and obtain what they need to address it? Can the organization change the artifact when its circumstances change? Is there an agreed way to retire the system if maintaining it ceases to make sense?

The period after the researchers leave can reveal a great deal about the design. It shows which knowledge and resources have actually become available to the people who will live with the result. A successful handover can be an important achievement of a research project, even if it is difficult to represent in the paper's original comparison table.
