# A Portfolio of Unfinished Work

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** O. Careers, institutions, and the economics of expertise | **Candidate tags:** career-advice, academia

The artifact in a portfolio is usually the result of decisions that its viewer cannot see. Sometimes the most revealing artifact is a prototype that the designer stopped using after discovering a problem in the original idea. I would like to understand what the discarded work taught them.

Suppose a designer presents a polished prototype. It is difficult to tell from the prototype alone which alternatives were considered, which constraint was important, or which decisions the designer made after seeing someone use it. The history of the project may reveal an understanding that the finished object does not require its viewer to notice.

An unfinished project can be particularly useful in that respect. Perhaps the person discovered that the original problem was poorly framed. Perhaps they found a much better approach but lacked the time to investigate it. Perhaps they simply made a series of mistakes. These are different accounts, and the distinction matters when we are trying to understand what they learned.

When polished results are easy to obtain, an evaluator may have a stronger reason to examine how a potential collaborator arrived at a decision. I would be cautious, though, about treating an elaborate process record as proof of individual expertise. Such records can also be generated, and a long project can contain very little useful inquiry.

A useful process record should make it possible to examine the relation between a discarded alternative and the decision that followed. A drawing of an abandoned mechanism, for example, could reveal which constraint the designer learned to recognize. The amount of unfinished work does not establish the quality of that learning, and a persuasive account of a decision still needs to be evaluated against the evidence available.

A portfolio becomes more informative when a reader can recognize a particular moment at which evidence changed a decision. The finished work may be the most useful record of that decision. An abandoned design may make the change clearer. What I would like to understand is how the person learned to see the problem differently.
