# Who Owns the Experiment?

**Status:** Long-form editorial manuscript | **Theme:** The institutions of knowledge | **Candidate tags:** sociology-of-science, academia, ai

Suppose a researcher has access to an assistant capable of proposing a useful experiment. The assistant can read the relevant literature, explain the proposed method, and write a program that would help conduct the experiment. The researcher needs something else before they can learn whether the idea works. They need access to the world in which the experiment can take place.

This is easy to recognize in a field that depends on a particular piece of equipment. A person can have an interesting idea about the behavior of a material while lacking a way to prepare and examine the material. A roboticist can develop an idea for a controller without having a suitable robot on which to investigate it.

I expect the ability to carry out experiments to become a particularly important part of the distribution of knowledge as AI becomes more capable. The cost of proposing and implementing an idea may fall much faster than the cost of obtaining the evidence through which the idea can be understood.

## The point at which an idea reaches an instrument

A useful example is [Autonomous chemical research with large language models](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06792-0), published by Boiko and colleagues in 2023. Their Coscientist system combines a language model with tools for gathering information, executing code, and controlling experimental equipment. The work investigates several kinds of tasks, including the planning and execution of chemical experiments and the use of existing experimental data in an optimization problem.

The importance of the example for this discussion is the connection between the model's ability to formulate an action and the infrastructure through which that action can take place. A model that provides instructions for an experiment supplies a useful capability. A model connected to appropriately prepared equipment can participate in a different activity: obtaining new evidence.

That difference matters when we talk about making research accessible. Giving many people a model with excellent scientific knowledge can make many activities easier. It does not necessarily give those people the same opportunity to determine whether an account of a physical phenomenon is correct.

A laboratory needs equipment, materials, maintenance, and people or systems capable of ensuring that operations are conducted appropriately. It has to decide which experiments may be performed, how failures will be handled, and how the resulting information can be used. An autonomous experimental system may reduce the amount of labor required for some of those operations. It still exists within an arrangement that makes the experiments possible.

This is a familiar problem in science. There have long been questions that could be investigated by only a small number of institutions. I think automation may change the relative importance of those arrangements in several fields at once.

## The scientist who can afford to try again

Consider two groups investigating the same question about a robotic system. Each group has access to capable software for designing controllers. One group has several robots, a prepared environment, and a way to conduct and record many trials. The other has one robot that needs to be shared among several projects.

The groups may be equally capable of describing interesting approaches. Their opportunities to discover something through testing those approaches differ. The first can investigate many candidates, learn from unexpected behavior, and return to promising alternatives. The second needs to decide carefully which trials it can afford.

The difference becomes more consequential when an agent can propose and revise candidates very quickly. A laboratory able to execute many useful experiments can take advantage of the agent's capacity to explore. The other laboratory may have to leave much of that capacity unused.

We can think of this as a relationship between intellectual and experimental resources. A useful experiment requires some combination of a proposal, an appropriate intervention, an observation, and an account of what the observation means. Reducing the cost of one part can make the conditions governing the other parts much more visible.

This will not happen uniformly. Simulators can provide useful substitutes for some physical experiments. Equipment can become less expensive, and robotic systems can make better use of a limited number of trials. Shared facilities can give several groups access to instruments that none could maintain alone. These changes could substantially broaden the range of people able to investigate difficult questions.

The distribution of the ability to experiment is therefore something worth observing. We should know whether new tools are making useful trials more accessible to many people or allowing a few institutions to conduct vastly more trials.

## What does the owner get to decide?

Ownership of an experimental environment can carry influence beyond deciding who may use it. The people operating the environment choose which properties it can measure, which interventions it supports, and what it records about the outcomes.

Imagine a facility for testing robots that is organized around reliably moving objects from one place to another. A researcher interested in that behavior can conduct an impressive range of experiments. Another researcher might want to examine how a person experiences sharing space with a robot. They may need a different environment, different observations, and procedures for involving people in the research.

The first facility can be exceptionally capable without supporting the questions of the second researcher. Its organization expresses a set of decisions about the kinds of inquiry for which it should provide infrastructure.

As research automation improves, those decisions may have greater consequences. An institution can make it inexpensive to investigate a question for which it has prepared an evaluator and an experimental environment. It can leave other questions comparatively expensive to explore. The range of possible inquiries will depend partly on what someone has chosen to make easy.

Another form of influence concerns access to the evidence. A research group may obtain a result using a facility that other groups cannot use. The result can be valuable. Other researchers may have difficulty challenging its assumptions, independently reproducing the conditions, or investigating a different explanation of what happened.

There is no requirement that every institution make all of its infrastructure available to everyone. Some experiments require careful restrictions, and maintaining equipment is expensive. The question is how a field organizes opportunities for independent inquiry when some of its most consequential observations depend on facilities that few people can access.

## A more open arrangement is possible

I can imagine a much more encouraging direction for the same technological development. An automated laboratory may make the use of scientific instruments considerably less dependent on being physically present with someone who knows how to operate them. Several universities and communities could share experimental services. Small groups could have opportunities to examine questions that would previously have required building a laboratory of their own.

Software for designing experiments could also make such facilities easier to use for people with less experience. They would still need appropriate guidance and safeguards. They could gain a useful way to investigate an idea without acquiring every technical ability involved in the operation of the equipment.

The scientific benefit would be particularly large if the facilities preserved meaningful opportunities to challenge results and pursue new questions. Researchers would need to know the conditions under which the instruments operate, what their observations represent, and which modifications they can request. Shared access to a fixed set of benchmark experiments would provide a different opportunity from access to a flexible environment that supports independent inquiry.

We should examine which of these arrangements develops. I would look for evidence about the price and availability of trials, who controls the choice of experiments, and whether researchers outside the institutions that own the strongest systems can obtain new evidence through them.

I expect automated research to make ideas and candidate solutions increasingly plentiful. The ability to test them in meaningful settings will remain an important part of scientific opportunity. We can use the technology to distribute that opportunity more widely. We can also organize it in a way that gives extraordinary investigative capacity to a relatively small number of institutions.

The answer will help determine not only who gets to do science, but which questions become inexpensive enough to ask.
