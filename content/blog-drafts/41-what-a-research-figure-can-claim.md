# What a Research Figure Can Claim

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** G. Writing, explanation, and representation | **Candidate tags:** computer-science, philosophy-of-science

A line rises steadily across a plot. Before reading the caption, we already have a sense that something has improved. But we do not yet know what changed along the horizontal axis, what was measured, or whether the line describes one experiment or the average of many.

A useful research figure helps the reader reconstruct the comparison. If several methods were tested on the same tasks, we should know whether the averages were computed over comparable cases. If one curve begins at a higher value, we should know whether the methods started from the same conditions. A line connecting points can make a trend look continuous even when the experiment observed only a few settings.

These details matter because readers often encounter the visual evidence before the full argument. An author can make an important pattern clear through the choice of axes and grouping. The same choices can make a limitation difficult to notice. It is possible to plot every measurement correctly and still lead a reader toward a conclusion that the data do not support.

I think of a figure as making a small claim in its own right. A caption should tell us enough to examine that claim, and the surrounding text should explain why the claim matters. This is different from asking the figure to prove the entire contribution of the paper.

As generating polished figures becomes easier, visual finish will tell us even less about the reasoning behind them. The work worth preserving is the relation between the observation, the comparison that the figure makes, and the conclusion we are allowed to draw.
