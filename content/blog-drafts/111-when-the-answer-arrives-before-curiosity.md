# When the Answer Arrives Before Curiosity

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** N. Learning, teaching, and shared practice | **Candidate tags:** education, philosophy-of-science

Someone watches a moving sculpture and wonders how it works. They can look behind it, move a part, or try to imagine what is hidden inside. An assistant could provide an accurate explanation as soon as they ask. Should it always do so?

An explanation can help a learner notice a relationship that they would otherwise miss. Its timing can also change the activity. A person who has just formed a question may enjoy investigating several possible answers. Receiving the solution immediately can bring that particular investigation to an end.

There are several ways a tutor could respond. It could answer directly, suggest a way to observe the mechanism, ask the learner what they think will happen next, or provide a hint about one part. Each can be useful depending on what the learner wants and what they already understand.

This suggests that we should pay attention to curiosity as something that develops through a sequence of encounters. A good educational system may need to recognize when someone wants information and when they want a chance to investigate. It may also help them become curious about something they had not noticed.

I would be careful about turning curiosity into a reason to withhold information. A learner who is frustrated or working toward another goal may benefit greatly from a direct explanation. The choice belongs to the educational purpose and the person's situation.

The abundance of answers gives us more freedom to decide when and how they should arrive. Sometimes the most valuable assistance will be an opportunity to develop a question before receiving its answer.

## Editorial source notes

Reading leads: inquiry learning, information-gap accounts of curiosity (Loewenstein, 1994), and research on guided discovery. Compare types of assistance in a specified task; do not assume that delay improves learning.
