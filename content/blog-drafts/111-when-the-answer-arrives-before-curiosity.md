# When the Answer Arrives Before Curiosity

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** N. Learning, teaching, and shared practice | **Candidate tags:** education, philosophy-of-science

An explanation can arrive before the learner has had a chance to develop the question that would make it useful. A moving sculpture offers a simple example. Someone can ask how it works and receive a correct description immediately, or they can examine its parts and begin to wonder why one part moves when another turns.

An explanation can help a learner notice a relationship that they would otherwise miss. Its timing can also change the activity. A person who has just formed a question may enjoy investigating several possible answers. Receiving the solution immediately can bring that particular investigation to an end.

There are several ways a tutor could respond. It could answer directly, suggest a way to observe the mechanism, ask the learner what they think will happen next, or provide a hint about one part. Each can be useful depending on what the learner wants and what they already understand.

A tutor could first ask whether the learner wants an explanation or a chance to investigate the mechanism. If the learner chooses to investigate, the tutor could suggest an observation that distinguishes two plausible accounts. The decision about when to supply an answer can then follow the activity the learner has chosen, rather than a fixed policy of delaying help.

The sequence of encounters matters because curiosity can develop during the investigation. A good educational system may need to recognize when someone wants information and when they want a chance to investigate. It may also help them become curious about something they had not noticed.

I would be careful about turning curiosity into a reason to withhold information. A learner who is frustrated or working toward another goal may benefit greatly from a direct explanation. The choice belongs to the educational purpose and the person's situation.

The important choice for a tutor is whether to provide information, help someone acquire an observation, or give them a chance to investigate an idea. A direct answer can be exactly what a learner needs. Sometimes a well-chosen hint makes the eventual explanation much more intelligible because the learner has already encountered the problem it addresses.
