# The Shape of a Tool Is a Social Decision

**Status:** editorial draft / argument development | **Theme:** Graphics, fabrication, and making | **Candidate tags:** participatory-design, ethics, computer-science

The shape of a tool can determine whether a person can take part in an activity independently. Imagine two handles for a shared piece of workshop equipment. One requires a strong grip in a particular position. The other allows several ways of applying the necessary force. Depending on the rest of the mechanism and the circumstances of use, those shapes can create different opportunities for participation.

The engineering question concerns which actions the handle permits and which forces it can safely transmit. The social question concerns who can perform the action, whose assistance they need, and what changes in the shared practice when another person becomes able to do it.

A tool can make a previously difficult operation accessible without making the entire activity accessible. Someone may still need help preparing the workpiece, adjusting the equipment, or recognizing an unsafe condition. Examining the complete activity can reveal which aspects of the new shape actually change a person's opportunities.

The difference is particularly visible in a shared activity. A person may be able to perform the operation after receiving a new handle, yet still have to ask someone else to set the equipment up. If the aim is independent participation, the setup is part of the relevant design. The handle's mechanical advantage alone cannot establish that the arrangement has changed.

Personal fabrication offers a particularly useful way to investigate such differences. Several variations of a tool can be produced for different bodies or practices. A maker can try a new shape and discover a way of working they had not considered. More capable computational design may make finding mechanically viable variations considerably less expensive.

There are important constraints. A customized handle has to satisfy the relevant mechanical and safety requirements. A collection of incompatible designs could make a shared workshop difficult to maintain. An alternative that looks accessible on a screen may require skills or assistance its designer has overlooked.

These conditions should inform the evaluation of the artifact rather than be treated as reasons to seek one shape that will serve everyone. A mechanical comparison can establish the properties of a set of designs. Observing how people use them can help us understand which relationships of assistance, independence, and participation the designs have changed.

I would like research on physical tools to take those relationships seriously. A shape can be an intervention in the organization of an activity, and that intervention deserves an account as precise as the account of its mechanical behavior.
