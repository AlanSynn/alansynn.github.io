# The Expert Who Makes the Tool Look Bad

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** K. Interaction, delegation, and responsibility | **Candidate tags:** participatory-design, education

An interface can look unnecessarily complicated when an experienced practitioner uses it. The steps that were supposed to guide the work may interrupt a sequence of decisions the person already knows how to make. An observation of this kind can expose assumptions about the task that were difficult to recognize while designing the tool.

This can be uncomfortable for a researcher who wants to demonstrate the advantages of a system. It can also be one of the most useful parts of studying experienced practitioners. The expert has developed ways of working around the constraints of their activity. A new tool reveals some of those ways when it fails to support them.

The same feature can be valuable to someone who is learning. A guided sequence of operations may make a complicated activity approachable, while requiring an expert to repeat decisions they have already made. We should understand the relation between the design and a user's developed practice before treating one workflow as universally desirable.

One useful comparison would let the expert use the new workflow and then ask which established operation became difficult to express. If the expert can accomplish the same operation after a small change in practice, the issue may concern learning the interface. If the representation cannot express a relationship central to the work, the design itself deserves reconsideration.

Comparing beginners and experienced users can reveal how their understanding of the activity shapes the usefulness of a feature. It can reveal different ways of understanding the task. A tool may need to support movement between those ways as someone learns.

I would examine what the expert accomplished through their earlier practice and whether the new tool provides another way to accomplish it. Sometimes the expert needs an opportunity to learn a different workflow. Sometimes the workflow needs to change. Observing their work can help us distinguish those cases before we decide that resistance is a problem to eliminate.
