# The Expert Who Makes the Tool Look Bad

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** K. Interaction, delegation, and responsibility | **Candidate tags:** participatory-design, education

An expert sometimes uses a new tool in a way that makes its design look unnecessarily complicated. They skip the steps that the designer thought were helpful. They use features together in unexpected ways. They may accomplish the task faster by returning to an older tool.

This can be uncomfortable for a researcher who wants to demonstrate the advantages of a system. It can also be one of the most useful parts of studying experienced practitioners. The expert has developed ways of working around the constraints of their activity. A new tool reveals some of those ways when it fails to support them.

The same feature can be valuable to someone who is learning. A guided sequence of operations may make a complicated activity approachable, while requiring an expert to repeat decisions they have already made. We should understand the relation between the design and a user's developed practice before treating one workflow as universally desirable.

This is why comparing beginners and experienced users can reveal more than a difference in performance. It can reveal different ways of understanding the task. A tool may need to support movement between those ways as someone learns.

Of course, experts can also be attached to practices that are no longer useful. Their resistance is not evidence by itself that a design is poor. The interesting question is what the old practice allowed them to do, which of those possibilities remain valuable, and how the new tool might support a different relationship to the same work.
