# When Evaluation Becomes an Algorithm

**Status:** Long-form editorial manuscript | **Theme:** Evidence after the scoreboard | **Candidate tags:** philosophy-of-science, ai, sociology-of-science

A researcher finds a way to make a program faster. She changes a small part of the implementation, runs the program again, and measures the difference. She has a better result and perhaps a better understanding of the system. Her next question concerns which change to try now.

If a machine can perform this sequence, the evaluation becomes part of a different kind of system. It can generate an implementation, execute it, compare the result, and retain promising changes. It can also search for better ways to choose the changes. A test that was once used to establish the quality of a research contribution has become one of the means of producing that contribution.

I expect this change to reach much further into research than we are currently comfortable admitting. AI will dominate quantified competition. A great deal of the work through which researchers have demonstrated their expertise will enter the same kind of improvement loop. That will change the authority of the score and, eventually, the kinds of contributions a field recognizes.

## The test moves inside the research process

The program at the beginning of this essay does not have to be easy to improve. Its implementation may be difficult to understand, and an improvement may require an ingenious new algorithm. These conditions make search difficult. They do not prevent a machine from participating in the search.

[AlphaEvolve](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.13131v1) gives a concrete example. Language models propose programs; evaluators assess them; the search uses information from earlier attempts to develop later ones. The authors describe applications ranging from mathematical problems to the optimization of computing infrastructure. One particularly interesting example concerns a scheduling heuristic used in Google's data centers. They report that the new heuristic recovers about 0.7 percent of fleet-wide computational resources on average after deployment. A small change can matter a great deal at that scale.

I find the relationship among those parts of the system more important for this discussion than the particular number. The researchers had a way to compare possible implementations. The agent could make use of that comparison and search for a better one. A result whose quality would once have been evidence of a specialist's skill can now be obtained through another arrangement.

An evaluation does not automatically make the underlying problem tractable. It can be expensive to carry out a test, and a particular evaluator can be a poor measure of the intended behavior. There may be few useful solutions and an enormous space of possibilities. In physical systems, a failed attempt may change the conditions for the next one. The speed with which different activities enter the loop will depend on such conditions.

Nevertheless, I would not place much confidence in the difficulty of a problem as a source of professional protection. An automated method does not need to solve the problem optimally. It needs to find useful results more reliably or economically than the people who would otherwise do the work. My forecast concerns the gradual satisfaction of the conditions that make that comparison favorable.

## What a better score tells us about its producer

A faster program has value. A person who finds a faster implementation may also gain professional recognition. In research, the two kinds of value have often been connected. A result that was difficult for others to obtain gave us a reason to infer something about the person who obtained it.

Suppose the implementation now comes from an agent that many researchers can use. Under comparable conditions, they can obtain a similar improvement. The improvement may continue to have exactly the same practical value. Its significance as evidence of a particular person's exceptional ability changes.

Research has always involved shared tools and the contributions of many people. A new automated method can also be a remarkable scientific contribution in its own right. A researcher may still discover a problem, develop a new way to investigate it, or create a system that others cannot reproduce. The social change I expect occurs as more parts of those activities become available as repeatable capabilities.

A conference may continue to ask for better scores after that change. But I expect increasing difficulty in justifying a system of professional recognition that depends on the ability to obtain them. If the same tools can continually produce better methods, the number can become a record of what a field's infrastructure can do. The result is useful to the field without necessarily offering the distinction the author once expected to receive from it.

It is possible for the opposite institutional effect to occur. The most powerful models, expensive experiments, and deployment environments may remain concentrated. Competition for a better result could grow more intense because it is a competition among organizations with different resources. Even in that case, a higher score may tell us increasingly more about the arrangement of resources than about a person's individual ability. Whether institutions change their interpretation of such results is an important part of the forecast.

## The movement that looked human

Consider research on gestures produced alongside speech. Someone makes a movement with their hand. The movement can look quite natural by itself. Whether it fits the sentence being spoken is another question.

The [GENEA Challenge 2022](https://doi.org/10.1145/3656374) investigated this distinction through controlled comparisons of generated gestures. The study separated ratings of human likeness from judgments of how well the gestures matched the accompanying speech. Some synthetic movements received higher human-likeness ratings than the recorded human movements used for comparison. The natural recordings retained a substantial advantage in appropriateness for the speech. Most of the automatic metrics investigated did not correlate reliably with the human-likeness judgments; one distributional metric performed better.

This was a quantitative study of perception. It did not establish that motion is impossible to evaluate numerically. The researchers made two aspects of the experience separately available for examination, and the results showed why the distinction mattered.

I am particularly interested in what happens when we carry the question into different activities. A gesture that looks appropriate in a formal presentation may feel excessive in a conversation between friends. An animator may deliberately produce an unnatural movement because of the character's personality. A person receiving physical assistance from a robot may care about qualities of movement that are difficult to appreciate from a video.

The value of a motion is often tied to what is happening around it. Human likeness, physical feasibility, and suitability for an expressive purpose give us different reasons to prefer an outcome. Even the same person can value different qualities when the purpose changes.

We can develop automated evaluations of many such properties. We can learn to predict an observer's preferences. I expect considerable progress here. What I doubt is the usefulness of treating the resulting evaluations as a universal order of motion quality. When the experiences and purposes differ, a better common number can conceal precisely the differences we want to understand.

## The person who wants to hear the robot

In [Understanding Expectations for a Robotic Guide Dog for Visually Impaired People](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.04594v1), Kim and colleagues studied how 18 blind and low-vision participants experienced several aspects of a quadrupedal guide robot. The researchers investigated its movement, communication, and explanations through interactions and feedback.

One part of the study compared two styles of walking. Most participants wanted a quieter robot. Some wanted to hear it, because the sound could help them locate the robot or recognize what it was doing. The desirable level of noise could also change with the surrounding environment. Participants differed in their preferences concerning resistance to their movements.

It is easy to imagine how a single preference score could be used to improve the robot. It is also easy to imagine a quieter robot becoming less useful to someone who was using sound as information. The difference belongs to the way that person experiences and interacts with the robot. A larger sample may help estimate how common the preference is. That estimate will not make the preference disappear.

This is a different problem from finding an accurate predictor of the average judgment. We might find an excellent predictor and still want to design different experiences for different people. We might discover through further interaction that users want to change the behavior in circumstances we did not initially consider.

There are qualities of a person's experience that I do not think a shared description can fully convey. I can explain that a movement felt reassuring or intrusive. The explanation gives another person a reason to attend to particular parts of the experience. It does not give that person the experience itself. This is my philosophical position, and it is separate from any result about the present limits of a preference model.

## Qualitative inquiry after common performance

Qualitative inquiry can give us access to distinctions that have not been part of a common evaluation. Researchers can observe what people do, explore their accounts of a situation, and introduce new possibilities through which their understanding can develop. In [Unpacking Non-Dualistic Design: The Soma Design Case](https://doi.org/10.1145/3462448), Höök and colleagues describe how bodily experience and the perspectives of other people enter a design process. The act of experiencing something can be part of the inquiry rather than merely a way to rate its final result.

We should not turn qualitative inquiry into another form of protected human labor. [Learning from human preferences](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2017/hash/d5e2c0adad503c91f91df240d0cd4e49-Abstract.html) already demonstrates that systems can learn objectives from people's comparative judgments. More capable systems may also assist with qualitative observations and interpretations. Much of what currently requires a researcher could become easier to automate.

My prediction concerns the structure of the field. As common performance becomes available, there will be less reason to force research about different experiences into a universal competition. More particular activities and preferences can become independent reasons to make and investigate something. A study may reveal why a particular group values a way of moving, or help someone develop an experience they had never been able to articulate.

This prediction can fail. The institutions of research may continue to allocate recognition through common rankings, even when the rankings primarily describe access to powerful infrastructure. Distribution platforms may favor a few standardized experiences over many smaller ones. A proliferation of qualitative work does not guarantee that its practitioners will have a livelihood.

But I expect the world of research to become more differentiated. Quantitative improvement will continue inside the machinery that produces results. The authority of the number as a general certificate of distinctive human achievement will weaken. What we have treated as personal differences will increasingly become the actual subjects of research.

A good score once gave a person a way to claim that their contribution stood above others. In many fields, I expect the machine to take over much of the work of making that claim possible. The more interesting world will be the one in which a particular experience can become worth investigating without having to defeat every other experience on the same scale.
