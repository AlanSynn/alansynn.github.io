# A Character Does Not Have to Look Human

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** H. Graphics, vision, and perception | **Candidate tags:** computer-science, philosophy-of-science

A wooden figure with a very simple body can make a surprisingly expressive movement. Its posture or timing may suggest something about a character even though its appearance has little in common with a person's body. I am interested in the possibility of working with these two kinds of resemblance separately.

Gunnar Johansson's classic work on biological motion studied the information carried by moving points associated with a person's body. The example shows how much a temporal relation can communicate when the visual representation is sparse. It does not tell us that every observer reads every movement in the same way, or that a small set of trajectories captures the entire experience of an action.

For animation and robotics, this opens an interesting distinction. A designer can choose how an object looks and how it moves, and those choices need not converge on the same notion of realism. A machine with a simple mechanical appearance may communicate a recognizable intention through its approach and timing. A highly realistic character can look strange if its movement does not fit what the viewer expects of it.

A useful comparison could retain the character's shape while changing the timing of a gesture, then retain the gesture while changing the shape. The resulting observations could help us understand which aspects of the interpretation depend on morphology and which on motion. They would not establish that a given movement has the same meaning for every viewer.

We should be careful when treating human likeness as a general purpose. A child designing a moving figure might want exaggerated motion. An artist might want a gesture that is deliberately mechanical. The useful question is what the motion communicates within the expressive form that the maker has chosen.

A designer working with an unusual body can investigate how that particular form makes a movement intelligible. The opportunity is to develop ways of expressing something through the relations among its parts, rather than evaluate every motion according to how closely it resembles the movement of a human body.
