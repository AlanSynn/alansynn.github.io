# An Organization That Cannot Learn From Its Agents

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** O. Careers, institutions, and the economics of expertise | **Candidate tags:** computer-science, sociology-of-science

An organization asks an agent to investigate several ways of improving a process. The agent produces a useful result, and the organization adopts it. A year later, another team encounters a related problem. They have access to the result but little understanding of the investigation through which it was obtained.

The organization may have become more productive without having become much better at learning about its own work. It has acquired a solution. The observations, rejected possibilities, and conditions that made the solution appropriate may have disappeared into a service or a temporary record.

This distinction matters when several projects need to build on one another. A team changing a process wants to know what earlier teams learned about it. If every new investigation begins by generating another solution, the organization may repeatedly pay for inquiries that could have informed one another.

Agents might help solve this problem by maintaining useful accounts of their work. A record of every interaction would not necessarily be useful. The organization needs to know which observations matter to future decisions, and which assumptions should be revisited when the circumstances change.

There is also an issue of control. A service provider may be able to improve its systems using information from many organizations. Individual organizations may have limited access to the representations through which those improvements were made.

I expect organizational learning to become an important question in the design of agentic systems. Obtaining a good answer is useful. Preserving enough understanding to investigate the next problem can be a different achievement.
