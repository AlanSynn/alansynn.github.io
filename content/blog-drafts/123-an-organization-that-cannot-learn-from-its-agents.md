# An Organization That Cannot Learn From Its Agents

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** O. Careers, institutions, and the economics of expertise | **Candidate tags:** computer-science, sociology-of-science

An agent delivers a successful improvement to an organization's workflow. A year later, the team that needs to investigate a similar problem can access the new workflow but little evidence of how it was developed. The organization has obtained a result without necessarily acquiring the understanding that could help it investigate another one.

That result can increase productivity immediately. The loss of the inquiry may become apparent only when the conditions change. It has acquired a solution. The observations, rejected possibilities, and conditions that made the solution appropriate may have disappeared into a service or a temporary record.

This distinction matters when several projects need to build on one another. A team changing a process wants to know what earlier teams learned about it. If every new investigation begins by generating another solution, the organization may repeatedly pay for inquiries that could have informed one another.

Agents might help solve this problem by maintaining useful accounts of their work. A record of every interaction would not necessarily be useful. The organization needs to know which observations matter to future decisions, and which assumptions should be revisited when the circumstances change.

A useful record might preserve why a previous change was introduced and the conditions under which the improvement was observed. If the underlying workload later changes, another team can examine whether the same explanation still applies. Keeping the decision without the observations that motivated it can leave the organization dependent on repeatedly reconstructing the problem.

There is also an issue of control. A service provider may be able to improve its systems using information from many organizations. Individual organizations may have limited access to the representations through which those improvements were made.

A useful organizational memory would preserve the observations and decisions whose meaning extends beyond the immediate task. A later team could see which assumptions were investigated and why a particular solution was appropriate at the time. That makes a delegated inquiry part of the organization's ability to learn, rather than only part of its capacity to get work done.
