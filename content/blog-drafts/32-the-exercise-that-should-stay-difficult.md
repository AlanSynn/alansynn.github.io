# The Exercise That Should Stay Difficult

**Status:** Long-form editorial manuscript | **Theme:** Education and the next generation of expertise | **Candidate tags:** education, philosophy-of-science

A student spends an hour trying to solve a problem. An AI assistant could solve it in a few seconds. If the purpose of the activity is to obtain the answer, it is difficult to justify making the student continue.

If the purpose of the activity is to help the student learn something, we have another question. What would the student learn by spending that hour on the problem? Could the assistant provide a useful form of help without taking away the part of the activity through which that learning occurs?

I think these questions will become increasingly important as we acquire systems that are capable of solving nearly every problem we give to a beginner. A great deal of difficulty can disappear from education. We should try to understand which difficulties we have good reasons to preserve.

## When the result and the learning separate

Suppose a learner is trying to write a small program that moves a character across a screen. The program behaves unexpectedly. The learner asks an assistant what is wrong, and the assistant corrects the code. The character now moves in the intended way.

If the learner is building a larger project, this can be an excellent use of assistance. They may want to spend their time designing the character's behavior and have little reason to investigate a problem in the programming environment.

In another setting, the problem could be part of learning how the program represents position over time. The student needs to understand why a particular operation has the observed effect. Obtaining the corrected code may allow them to complete the exercise without developing that understanding.

We can distinguish these purposes by considering what happens when the student encounters another example. If they have developed an understanding of the relevant operations, they may be able to anticipate what an unfamiliar program will do. If they have only learned to recognize a useful correction, they may need the same form of assistance again.

Both are useful capabilities. Someone who knows how to work with an assistant can do things they could not otherwise do. A person who understands a particular concept can use that understanding to approach many other problems. An educational activity should make clear which of these things it is trying to develop.

This is especially important when the output is used to evaluate learning. A good result from a collaboration between a student and a machine tells us something about what that arrangement can produce. It may provide little information about which parts of the work the student has come to understand.

## What productive failure means

I find the work of Manu Kapur on productive failure useful here. In [Productive Failure in Learning Math](https://doi.org/10.1111/cogs.12107), published in 2014, Kapur reports two randomized studies comparing approaches to learning mathematics. One approach gives students opportunities to attempt problems before receiving instruction. In the other, instruction precedes problem solving. Both arrangements supported procedural knowledge in the studied settings. Students who attempted problems first showed advantages on conceptual understanding and transfer to new problems.

The relevant point is not that being unable to solve a problem makes someone learn. The students subsequently received instruction. Their attempts gave them experiences and candidate explanations through which they could encounter the ideas introduced later.

Imagine learning a new way to organize a collection of numbers. A teacher could begin by showing us the procedure. We could follow it and produce the correct result. Alternatively, we could begin with a problem that makes us try several inadequate procedures. When the teacher introduces the new idea, we may have a reason to notice what those earlier procedures were missing.

This is a useful way to understand how the order of activities can affect learning. A particular difficulty can provide a learner with a question that makes an explanation meaningful.

The distinction is more subtle than a choice between direct instruction and unsupported discovery. In his [2016 discussion of productive and unproductive failure and success](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00461520.2016.1155457), Kapur examines how the quality of immediate performance and the quality of later learning can come apart. A successful attempt can support learning, and an unsuccessful one can leave the learner with very little. The design of the learning arrangement matters.

The evidence does not give us a general reason to make students struggle. It gives us a reason to examine what they can learn through a particular attempt and how they will be helped to develop that understanding afterward.

## Designing the help

An AI tutor could give a learner much more than a solution. It could help the learner form a question, suggest an observation, offer a smaller example, or point out a relationship that the learner had not noticed.

Consider the student whose character is not moving as expected. A useful response might ask what the student thinks will happen when one variable changes. The student could make a prediction, try the change, and examine the difference. The assistant could then help them connect the observation to the concept they are learning.

This could be a better experience than asking the learner to spend an hour searching for an error without guidance. A tutor with access to the learner's attempts might recognize that the student is investigating the wrong part of the problem, or that they need a more fundamental explanation before continuing.

There are also moments when a direct answer is the useful form of help. A student may have an interesting idea for a project and encounter a difficulty unrelated to the intended learning. A student may be unable to participate because the available representation does not work for them. Removing that obstacle can make the activity more educational.

A difficulty is worth preserving when it gives the learner a useful opportunity to develop the intended understanding. We should be able to explain what that opportunity is. It should be possible to reconsider the decision when the learner's circumstances change.

This is an interesting challenge for educational technology. We can investigate not only whether a form of assistance improves immediate performance but also whether it helps a learner develop a more useful account of the activity. It may be possible to do both. A system can help someone continue a project while occasionally creating opportunities to examine an idea that will matter to their later work.

## The future of a difficult exercise

A powerful assistant may change the reasons we have for asking students to do many of the things they currently do. Some familiar exercises may become unnecessary. Others may become important for reasons quite different from the ones through which they were originally justified.

A programming course might give students access to agents while they work on a substantial project, then organize particular activities around understanding how a program behaves. An engineering course might automate much of the calculation involved in developing a mechanism while providing learners with ways to examine the relationships among its parts.

We should also be prepared for the abilities worth developing to change. If a technical procedure becomes easily available, a learner may benefit more from understanding where it can be applied, what its assumptions are, or how to recognize an inappropriate result. There is little reason to preserve an entire curriculum merely because the activities in it were once difficult for an expert.

I expect education to become more deliberate about the relation between an exercise and the understanding it is supposed to help a person develop. The strongest reason to preserve a difficulty will be a clear account of the learning it makes possible, rather than the difficulty of the achievement itself.

Sometimes the exercise that takes an hour will deserve that hour. Sometimes the right thing to do will be to give the student the answer and let them move on to a more interesting question. A capable tutor can help with both. What matters is that we understand which part of the activity belongs to the education of the person who is doing it.
