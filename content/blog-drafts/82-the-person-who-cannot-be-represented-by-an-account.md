# The Person Who Cannot Be Represented by an Account

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** K. Interaction, delegation, and responsibility | **Candidate tags:** ethics, participatory-design

I might ask an assistant to be concise while I am working and enjoy a long explanation when I am learning something unfamiliar. These preferences belong to the same person. They make sense in different activities.

A personalized system often needs to construct a representation of its user. Some properties of that representation may remain stable. Others describe a temporary situation or a relationship with another person. The challenge is to keep those differences from becoming a single fixed account of who someone is.

Suppose a person often asks an agent to make decisions for them at work. The agent might infer that they prefer delegation. In a creative activity, the same person may want to make every small decision themselves. A useful assistant should know when its earlier inference is relevant, and when to leave the question open.

This matters for systems that carry information across many parts of a person's life. Persistent personalization can make interactions easier. It can also cause an assumption formed in one setting to follow the user into another. The person may not know which earlier activities have shaped the response they receive today.

I expect research on personalization to pay more attention to the scope of what a system has learned. A preference can be attached to a role, a task, a relationship, or a period of time. Letting people see and revise that scope may be as useful as improving the accuracy of the preference model. An account can support a person without having to become a comprehensive account of their life.
