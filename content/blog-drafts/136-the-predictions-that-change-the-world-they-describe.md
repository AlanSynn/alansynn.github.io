# The Predictions That Change the World They Describe

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** P. Knowledge, meaning, and life after common measures | **Candidate tags:** uncertainty, sociology-of-science, philosophy-of-science

A forecast about the disappearance of a profession can become one of the forces that changes the profession. Students decide what to study partly in response to it. Organizations may invest less in training people for the work. Years later, the outcome we wanted to predict has acquired a history in which the prediction itself played a role.

Forecasts about technology can influence the conditions under which they become true or false. A prediction of a shortage might lead an institution to invest in an activity it would otherwise have neglected. A prediction of rapid automation might lead people to reorganize a profession before the relevant technology is ready.

Predictions about careers are particularly difficult for this reason. Technical claims can sometimes be investigated under fairly stable conditions. The social consequences of the technology depend partly on how people respond to the expected changes. Those responses can vary between institutions, countries, and communities.

An apparently accurate prediction can therefore have more than one explanation. The technology might have displaced the work as predicted, or institutions might have withdrawn the opportunities through which people could enter the field before the technology was ready. These explanations have different implications for future decisions. An assessment of the prediction needs evidence about that intervening history.

I still think it is worth making strong predictions. They can help people examine assumptions that are otherwise difficult to question. But a useful prediction should make clear which mechanisms it expects to operate. If a forecast becomes true partly because people acted on it, we should be able to investigate that history rather than treating the outcome as straightforward confirmation of the original explanation.

There is also a responsibility involved in describing a future that other people will use to make decisions. We should make it possible for them to understand where our confidence comes from and which conditions might change. We should not turn a forecast into an instruction that everyone must organize their life around.

When looking back at a technological forecast, I would want to examine the decisions it helped bring about as well as the accuracy of its description of technical change. A prediction can be useful because it leads people to prevent an anticipated outcome. It can also become true through a mechanism the author did not understand. The future provides evidence about both the prediction and what people did with it.
