# When Productivity Leaves the Worker

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** O. Careers, institutions, and the economics of expertise | **Candidate tags:** sociology-of-science, career-advice

Suppose a small team develops a way to complete a week's work in a day. There are several things that could happen next. The team might receive more projects, spend more time investigating difficult problems, or work fewer hours. The organization might decide it needs fewer people.

The improvement in productivity does not determine which of these outcomes will occur. It changes what the organization can do with its resources. The arrangement through which those resources are allocated determines how the people involved experience the change.

This is one of the difficulties of using aggregate productivity as a reason to expect better working lives. A person may gain access to a tool that makes their work considerably easier. At the same time, their position in the organization may become less secure because the organization no longer needs as much of that work.

The relationship can change again if the new capability creates demand for other activities. An inexpensive way of producing software might make projects feasible that nobody was previously willing to fund. Some people may find new work in those projects. Others may be unable to move into the resulting roles.

I expect the future of work to involve all of these mechanisms, often within the same field. We need to observe who controls the improved means of production, which activities acquire new demand, and how the gains are distributed. The ability to get more done is a genuine achievement. It is too little information to tell us what will happen to the people whose work has become more productive.
