# When Productivity Leaves the Worker

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** O. Careers, institutions, and the economics of expertise | **Candidate tags:** sociology-of-science, career-advice

A team that can complete a week's work in a day has gained a useful capability. Whether the people in that team gain a shorter working week, higher pay, more demanding projects, or fewer colleagues depends on the arrangements through which the organization uses the improvement. Productivity and the distribution of its benefits are different questions.

The improvement in productivity does not determine which of these outcomes will occur. It changes what the organization can do with its resources. The arrangement through which those resources are allocated determines how the people involved experience the change.

The distinction matters when aggregate productivity is offered as a reason to expect better working lives. A person may gain access to a tool that makes their work considerably easier. At the same time, their position in the organization may become less secure because the organization no longer needs as much of that work.

The relationship can change again if the new capability creates demand for other activities. An inexpensive way of producing software might make projects feasible that nobody was previously willing to fund. Some people may find new work in those projects. Others may be unable to move into the resulting roles.

Consider two organizations that obtain the same reduction in the labor required for a service. One faces growing demand and uses the additional capacity to serve more people. The other has little opportunity to expand and reduces its staffing. A measurement of the productivity improvement alone cannot distinguish the consequences for employment. Demand and the distribution of control over the resulting capacity matter.

A technological advance changes the range of arrangements an organization can afford. It leaves the distribution of the gains to decisions about ownership, demand, working time, and bargaining power. A forecast about people's lives needs an account of those decisions. A measurement of the improvement in output is only the first piece of evidence.
