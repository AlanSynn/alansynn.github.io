# The Person Behind the Preference

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** P. Knowledge, meaning, and life after common measures | **Candidate tags:** ethics, participatory-design

A person says that they prefer to do something themselves. A system could learn the preference and arrange for them to do it. We might consider the interaction successful. There is another interesting question about what the activity means to that person.

Imagine someone who chooses to repair a small object that belonged to their family. They may enjoy working with their hands. They may also feel a responsibility toward an object whose history matters to them. Another person might prefer to pay a skilled worker to carry out the same repair because they want to preserve it carefully. Both choices can express a similar attachment.

An account of preference can help a system choose a useful action. An account of someone's reasons can tell us something about the commitments through which their choices make sense. We should distinguish the two, especially when a person's choices change.

A system that learns a regular pattern of behavior may have difficulty interpreting a decision that appears to contradict the pattern. The decision may be understandable through a relationship or responsibility the earlier observations did not reveal. Asking for more examples of the original behavior would not necessarily help.

Of course, people should not have to disclose their reasons whenever they want to make a choice. A useful technology can often accommodate a preference without investigating the person's life. The opportunity is to recognize when an explanation would help support the activity, and when respecting a choice is sufficient.

I expect systems to become excellent at learning many of our preferences. The design question of how much they should learn about the reasons behind them will remain tied to the relationships we are willing to establish with those systems.
