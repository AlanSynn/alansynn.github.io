# What the Sketch Was Trying to Say

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** I. Craft, mechanisms, and fabrication | **Candidate tags:** participatory-design, computer-science

A sketch of a movement is often a good enough way to begin a design. It is less clear that it is a good enough specification of the design. Someone may draw the path of a character's hand before knowing how quickly the hand should move, what the movement should express, or how it could be realized mechanically.

In [MotionSmith](https://doi.org/10.1145/3772318.3791545), we encountered this distinction through our work with automata artists. The system could generate mechanisms that approximated a sketched motion and indicate their accuracy. In the final design sessions, the artists generally attended more to how the movement looked and how the mechanism could be changed. Their motion goals also developed as they worked.

The sketch had served its purpose. It gave the maker and the computer something with which to begin. But a mechanism that follows the sketch more closely need not be the mechanism the maker ultimately wants. Watching an alternative can change the person's understanding of the original idea.

This is a particularly important distinction for generative tools. If a person describes something incompletely, the system needs to decide which parts of the description to treat as fixed and which to treat as invitations to explore. A highly capable system may interpret an ambiguous input well. The maker may nevertheless discover a different direction through the result.

The design of a creative system involves deciding when an input should become an obligation. A sketch can express a constraint, record a possibility, or help a person discover an intention. The person should be able to move among those roles without starting the project again.
