# A Conversation Is More Than Its Transcript

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** G. Writing, explanation, and representation | **Candidate tags:** participatory-design, philosophy-of-science

A transcript can preserve the words of an interview without preserving all the evidence available to someone who was present. There may have been a long silence before an answer, or a gesture that changed how another person understood it. Those observations can matter to an interpretation of what the conversation accomplished.

A researcher working with a transcript has access to useful evidence. A researcher who was present may remember details that make a particular exchange easier to interpret. An audio or video recording can preserve more of those details. Each source makes some observations available and leaves others uncertain.

A pause is evidence that a pause occurred. Inferring hesitation, disagreement, or reflection from it requires other observations. A subsequent response from the speaker, or the way the exchange developed, might help distinguish those interpretations. Recording more channels does not eliminate the work of interpretation.

The difference matters when a group asks an agent to summarize a meeting. The summary may identify the proposals, decisions, and unresolved questions correctly. It may still fail to reflect that one person tried repeatedly to raise a concern and eventually stopped. A record of the accepted decisions and an account of the process through which they were accepted serve different purposes.

Recording more of a conversation is not always the answer. People may speak differently when recorded, and participants have reasons to limit the collection or later use of their interactions. A richly documented exchange can carry costs that a transcript avoids. We need to understand what the inquiry requires before deciding what should be preserved.

When a system summarizes an exchange, I would like to know which parts of the exchange were available to it and what kind of account it has produced. A record of decisions can be excellent for continuing the work. A question about whose concerns were heard may require an account of the interaction through which the decisions were reached.

## Editorial source notes

Reading leads: conversation analysis, Erving Goffman's interaction order, and research on automated meeting summaries. Use consented recordings or published examples. Distinct from simulated users: focus on the evidence left by an actual exchange.
