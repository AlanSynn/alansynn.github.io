# What We Lose When We Lose a Skill

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** P. Knowledge, meaning, and life after common measures | **Candidate tags:** history-of-science, education, career-advice

A machine can replace a traditional way of producing an object without reproducing the activity through which someone acquired the relevant skill. What disappears when the activity becomes economically unnecessary depends on the role it played in the lives of the people who practiced it.

Some of the knowledge may remain useful. A maker may understand how to recognize a failure, modify an unusual component, or explain the relation between several movements. Other parts may have little practical use once the new method becomes common. The activity can still be an occasion through which people develop a way of noticing the world.

The economic value of a skill and its role in a practice can change independently. A skill is sometimes a way of producing an outcome that can be obtained through other means. It can also be part of a tradition, a form of education, or a practice people enjoy sharing. Losing the economic reason to acquire a skill can make it difficult to sustain those other activities.

There are limits to what we can preserve. Every generation inherits practices that have become difficult to justify maintaining. People should have room to choose which ones remain meaningful rather than feeling obliged to reproduce the work of their predecessors.

Preserving a technique in a recording and sustaining a practice among people are different arrangements. A video might help someone reconstruct an operation many years later. A community that continues the activity also provides opportunities to encounter variations, learn from others, and develop new reasons to use it. Which arrangement deserves support depends on what we value about the practice.

The opportunity created by abundant capabilities may be to make some of these choices more deliberate. We can use new tools to learn about older processes, document them, and support communities that want to continue them. Whether those communities have the time and resources to do so is a separate question.

The question for a community is which of those roles it still has a reason to support. A practice may survive as a means of learning, an enjoyable activity, or a shared way of understanding materials. Another may reasonably disappear. We should be able to make those decisions with an understanding of what is being relinquished, rather than infer from the availability of the finished object that every part of the earlier practice has been preserved.

## Editorial source notes

Reading leads: histories of craft and automation, Richard Sennett, The Craftsman (2008), and situated accounts of skill acquisition. Distinct from When the Field Moves: focus on preserving or losing a practice as cultural knowledge rather than a researcher's adaptation.
