# What We Lose When We Lose a Skill

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** P. Knowledge, meaning, and life after common measures | **Candidate tags:** history-of-science, education, career-advice

A person learns to make a mechanical object using tools that have been familiar to makers for many years. Later, a machine can produce the same object much more easily. It is reasonable for people to choose the machine. What happens to the knowledge involved in the older practice?

Some of the knowledge may remain useful. A maker may understand how to recognize a failure, modify an unusual component, or explain the relation between several movements. Other parts may have little practical use once the new method becomes common. The activity can still be an occasion through which people develop a way of noticing the world.

I think this deserves attention when we discuss the disappearance of skills. A skill is sometimes a way of producing an outcome that can be obtained through other means. It can also be part of a tradition, a form of education, or a practice people enjoy sharing. Losing the economic reason to acquire a skill can make it difficult to sustain those other activities.

There are limits to what we can preserve. Every generation inherits practices that have become difficult to justify maintaining. People should have room to choose which ones remain meaningful rather than feeling obliged to reproduce the work of their predecessors.

The opportunity created by abundant capabilities may be to make some of these choices more deliberate. We can use new tools to learn about older processes, document them, and support communities that want to continue them. Whether those communities have the time and resources to do so is a separate question.

What disappears with a skill depends on the role the skill played in people's lives. We should understand that role before concluding that the availability of an easier method has preserved everything that mattered.

## Editorial source notes

Reading leads: histories of craft and automation, Richard Sennett, The Craftsman (2008), and situated accounts of skill acquisition. Distinct from When the Field Moves: focus on preserving or losing a practice as cultural knowledge rather than a researcher's adaptation.
