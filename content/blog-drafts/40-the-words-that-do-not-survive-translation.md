# The Words That Do Not Survive Translation

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** G. Writing, explanation, and representation | **Candidate tags:** education, philosophy-of-science

A translation can preserve the apparent content of a sentence while altering the relationship between the people speaking. A brief response that suggests familiarity in one situation may be interpreted as cold in another. Choosing an equivalent expression involves deciding which features of the original interaction matter.

Language allows us to carry part of a situation to someone who was not there. It rarely carries all the expectations through which the original speakers understood it. Sometimes an expression makes sense because two people know each other well. An explanation for an unfamiliar reader may need to make that history visible.

The difficulty becomes interesting when a model offers a translation that reads perfectly well in both languages. The words seem to correspond, yet a speaker recognizes that something about their relation to the listener has changed. Improving literal accuracy may not resolve the difference. There may be several translations that preserve different things.

A translation could preserve the content of a request while making it sound like a command. A reader interested in the information being conveyed may consider the translation successful. A speaker concerned with the relationship established through the request may reject it. A useful comparison would let the two purposes remain visible.

The ambiguity gives us a particular question about translation tools. We could study the choices people make when they want an expression to travel across languages, and the choices they reject because those expressions would change the relationship. A tool might help people compare different ways of speaking rather than quietly selecting a single equivalent.

A useful translation tool could help someone examine several ways of carrying an expression into another language and understand what each preserves. It could make the relationship between meaning, register, and situation more available to the speaker. That would offer a different kind of assistance from silently choosing the sentence that seems most likely to be accepted.
