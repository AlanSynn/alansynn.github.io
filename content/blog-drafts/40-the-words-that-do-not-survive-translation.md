# The Words That Do Not Survive Translation

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** G. Writing, explanation, and representation | **Candidate tags:** education, philosophy-of-science

Someone can translate the words of a conversation accurately and still change what the conversation feels like. Consider a person who gives a brief answer in a setting where speaking directly suggests familiarity. In another setting, the same words might sound cold. A translator has to choose which aspects of the exchange to preserve.

Language allows us to carry part of a situation to someone who was not there. It rarely carries all the expectations through which the original speakers understood it. Sometimes an expression makes sense because two people know each other well. An explanation for an unfamiliar reader may need to make that history visible.

The difficulty becomes interesting when a model offers a translation that reads perfectly well in both languages. The words seem to correspond, yet a speaker recognizes that something about their relation to the listener has changed. Improving literal accuracy may not resolve the difference. There may be several translations that preserve different things.

This suggests a research problem beyond translation quality in the usual sense. We could study the choices people make when they want an expression to travel across languages, and the choices they reject because those expressions would change the relationship. A tool might help people compare different ways of speaking rather than quietly selecting a single equivalent.

I would not conclude that communication across languages is impossible. People learn to recognize one another's habits, explain their intentions, and create new shared meanings. The interesting question is how much of that work remains visible when a fluent system appears to have removed the need for it.
