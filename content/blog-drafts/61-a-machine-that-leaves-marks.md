# A Machine That Leaves Marks

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** I. Craft, mechanisms, and fabrication | **Candidate tags:** computer-science, participatory-design

Marks left by a cutting tool can become part of an object's appearance. A maker may want to remove them to obtain a uniform surface, or intentionally arrange them into a pattern. The manufacturing process has become one of the things the person can use to express an idea.

A manufacturing mark can carry information about the process through which something was made. Sometimes it is an unwanted defect. Sometimes it helps someone understand the material or gives the object a quality they like. A mark can be valued independently of the dimensional accuracy of the part.

A tool designed to remove every manufacturing trace would offer fewer expressive possibilities in this case. Imagine an artist who wants the path of a cutting tool to become part of a pattern. The artist needs access to choices that a conventional workflow may hide as implementation details. The trajectory of the machine becomes a part of the expressive medium.

There are important limits. A rough surface can interfere with a joint or create a hazard. A variation that looks interesting may undermine the object's strength. Artistic choices and mechanical requirements can coexist, and the system needs to help the maker understand where they interact.

The relevant design variables can belong to the process as well as the surface. Two parts with the same outline might acquire different appearances because the cutting paths differ. If the maker wants to control the traces, the interface needs to communicate what can be changed in the process without compromising the dimensions the assembly requires.

I would like fabrication tools to make these choices available where the activity calls for them. A maker can treat the path of the cutting tool as something to compose, subject to the material and safety requirements of the object. What counts as a desirable finish then depends on the purpose for which the surface is being made.
