# A Machine That Leaves Marks

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** I. Craft, mechanisms, and fabrication | **Candidate tags:** computer-science, participatory-design

A machine cuts a shape from wood. When the part is finished, there are small marks left by the path of the cutting tool. Another manufacturing process could produce a shape that looks smoother. Whether that is an improvement depends on what the maker wants from the object.

A manufacturing mark can carry information about the process through which something was made. Sometimes it is an unwanted defect. Sometimes it helps someone understand the material or gives the object a quality they like. A mark can be valued independently of the dimensional accuracy of the part.

This complicates the idea of a fabrication tool that always produces the cleanest possible result. Imagine an artist who wants the path of a cutting tool to become part of a pattern. The artist needs access to choices that a conventional workflow may hide as implementation details. The trajectory of the machine becomes a part of the expressive medium.

There are important limits. A rough surface can interfere with a joint or create a hazard. A variation that looks interesting may undermine the object's strength. Artistic choices and mechanical requirements can coexist, and the system needs to help the maker understand where they interact.

The opportunity is to reconsider which aspects of fabrication belong to the maker's vocabulary. Once a machine can reliably create many shapes, researchers can explore the relations among the shape, the process, and the experience of making it. A result that once appeared to be evidence of poor manufacturing may become a deliberate part of the work.
