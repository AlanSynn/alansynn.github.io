# The Lesson That Continues Tomorrow

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** N. Learning, teaching, and shared practice | **Candidate tags:** education, computer-science

At the end of a class, a half-built mechanism and a saved design file can describe two different states of the same project. The student has also been thinking about a movement they want to change. Tomorrow's lesson begins with the work of bringing those three things back into relation.

They need to remember which part they were adjusting, what they noticed about the movement, and which idea they wanted to try next. The physical object may have changed since the design was saved. Pieces may have been stored in another place. A large part of resuming the work involves finding the relation between these things.

For a tool intended to support classroom making, continuity can therefore become a design concern. Saving the digital model is useful. So are storing the physical pieces, recording unfinished operations, and helping a student recognize the state of their project after an interruption.

The record needed for tomorrow might be surprisingly small: the joint the student was adjusting, the relation they wanted to preserve, and the result of the last attempt. Saving every interaction can make the important information harder to find. A useful record is one whose contents correspond to the decisions through which the student will resume the activity.

The same problem can arise across longer periods of independent learning. An agent might remember the sequence of commands through which a student created something. The student may instead need an account of why they chose a particular direction and what they have not yet understood. The relevant memory depends on the next educational activity.

The ability to resume work can change which kinds of lesson are feasible. A project that is difficult to resume favors activities that can be completed in one sitting. A system that helps people return to unfinished work can support a different kind of exploration.

A saved file preserves a particular representation. A well-supported return to a project lets the learner recognize what they were investigating and make their next decision. That can allow a classroom to undertake inquiries whose value depends on several days of exploration, rather than arranging every meaningful task around the length of a class period.
