# The Lesson That Continues Tomorrow

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** N. Learning, teaching, and shared practice | **Candidate tags:** education, computer-science

A student stops working on a mechanism when the class period ends. Several parts are assembled, and others are lying nearby. On the computer, there is a design file. Tomorrow, the student needs to recover the project as an activity rather than simply open the file.

They need to remember which part they were adjusting, what they noticed about the movement, and which idea they wanted to try next. The physical object may have changed since the design was saved. Pieces may have been stored in another place. A large part of resuming the work involves finding the relation between these things.

For a tool intended to support classroom making, continuity can therefore become a design concern. Saving the digital model is useful. So are storing the physical pieces, recording unfinished operations, and helping a student recognize the state of their project after an interruption.

The same problem can arise across longer periods of independent learning. An agent might remember the sequence of commands through which a student created something. The student may instead need an account of why they chose a particular direction and what they have not yet understood. The relevant memory depends on the next educational activity.

This is an example of a small feature that can change the structure of a lesson. A project that is difficult to resume favors activities that can be completed in one sitting. A system that helps people return to unfinished work can support a different kind of exploration.

The lesson may end at a particular time. The student's inquiry can continue when the environment gives them a meaningful way to return to it.
