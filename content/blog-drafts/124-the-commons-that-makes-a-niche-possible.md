# The Commons That Makes a Niche Possible

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** O. Careers, institutions, and the economics of expertise | **Candidate tags:** sociology-of-science, computer-science, ethics

A small community may have a reason to maintain a very particular tool, but little reason to build every component of that tool itself. It can use a common programming language, shared libraries, and a platform through which its members exchange their work. The existence of those shared resources changes which activities the community can sustain.

This is an important part of the promise of abundant computational ability. A person can use a general tool to build something for a specific practice. If the practice shares some of the costs of maintaining its infrastructure with other communities, its small size need not make the project impossible.

The interesting question is which parts of the arrangement need to remain common. A standard file format may let people exchange objects among specialized programs. A shared fabrication service may make it possible for a community to produce unusual parts. Each community can develop different values and still benefit from some of the same infrastructure.

Those resources have costs. Someone must maintain them, make decisions about changes, and respond when users have conflicting needs. A provider that initially supports many small communities may later find it profitable to favor the ones that generate the most revenue.

I expect some of the diversity enabled by AI to depend on how well we organize this shared layer. A community's ability to develop its own practice need not require complete independence. It may require access to common resources whose future it can reasonably trust and, where appropriate, help shape.
