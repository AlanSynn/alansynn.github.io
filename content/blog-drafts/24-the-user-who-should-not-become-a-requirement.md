# The User Who Should Not Become a Requirement

**Status:** editorial draft / argument development | **Theme:** HCI, institutions, and value | **Candidate tags:** participatory-design, philosophy-of-science, sociology-of-science

HCI papers often conclude a study of people's lives with a set of design implications. It is an understandable format. It can also make the most interesting result of an inquiry look like an incomplete list of features.

## Working argument

Paul Dourish's 2006 essay on implications for design challenges the idea that ethnographic contributions should be evaluated primarily by the requirements they deliver. The deeper achievement of an ethnography may be the account through which a familiar practice becomes intelligible. A study can alter our understanding of the categories of user, task, or context before it gives anyone a feature to implement.

This distinction becomes sharper when agents make implementing features cheap. A team can read interview summaries, produce many prototypes, and select the ones that test well. A serious misunderstanding of the practice can survive that productive process. Imagine a workplace whose informal exceptions protect workers from an unreasonable scheduling rule. Translating the practice into a requirement for faster exception handling may preserve the organizational problem.

The essay would argue that some qualitative results deserve to remain explanatory, critical, or generative. They can inform several incompatible designs, justify leaving a practice alone, or change the question a project asks. The result is not necessarily exhausted by a list of actionable recommendations. That is a substantive claim about the function of knowledge, rather than an excuse for vagueness.

## Challenge

Design implications can be valuable, and theoretical accounts also require evidence. Explain the analytic contribution clearly and show what observations support it. Avoid presenting every refusal to provide a recommendation as intellectual depth.
