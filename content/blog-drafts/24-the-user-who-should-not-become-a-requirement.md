# The User Who Should Not Become a Requirement

**Status:** Long-form editorial manuscript | **Theme:** HCI, institutions, and value | **Candidate tags:** participatory-design, philosophy-of-science, sociology-of-science

A good account of people's lives can change our understanding of what needs to be designed, including whether a system should be designed at all. I am concerned about the expectation that qualitative research must turn that understanding into a list of useful features.

Imagine a shared workshop with a schedule for using its equipment. Members often exchange reserved periods. One gives up time to help a beginner, another accommodates a member who needs to finish a project. An efficient scheduling tool might make those exchanges easier. Before building it, I would like to understand what the existing exchanges do for the people involved. They may be occasions through which members recognize one another's circumstances and maintain a relationship. A system that changes the exchanges could change more of the practice than the schedule.

## When understanding is expected to produce a feature

In his 2006 CHI paper, [Implications for Design](https://www.pauldourish.com/publications/2006/implications-chi2006.pdf), Paul Dourish examines the expectation that ethnographic studies in HCI should produce recommendations for designing systems. He argues that judging ethnographic work mainly by the requirements it provides can fail to recognize the kind of knowledge such an investigation produces.

It is easy to understand how the expectation developed. HCI involves the creation of technology. We study people's activities partly because we want to understand how a tool could be useful to them. A design implication can give a reader a concrete way to see the connection between the study and a possible technological contribution.

The difficulty lies in treating that connection as the main evidence that the inquiry was valuable. An ethnography can change our understanding of an activity, including the categories through which we understand it. That change may matter to the design of many systems. It may also give us a reason to question the project through which the study was originally motivated.

Consider the workshop again. We might observe that members frequently exchange reserved periods. It would be reasonable to develop a feature through which they can do so. If we understand the exchanges only as adjustments to a schedule, however, we may miss their significance as part of a social arrangement.

Perhaps members have developed a way to recognize one another's circumstances. A person who needs more time can explain why, and others can decide what they are willing to do. The informal exchange can become an occasion through which people maintain a relationship. A more efficient scheduling system may change the occasions on which those relationships develop.

These are possible interpretations of a hypothetical practice. We would need to investigate the actual workshop to determine whether they are useful. The important point is that the interpretations can lead us toward substantially different questions about the proposed technology.

## The person we met and the person in the specification

A requirement often expresses something a system should allow, prevent, or accomplish. This makes it useful in coordinating work. Several people can build different parts of a system while sharing an understanding of what those parts are expected to do.

Moving from an observation of a person's life to a requirement involves a choice about what the observation means. Suppose we discover that members of the workshop sometimes prefer not to tell others why they need access to the equipment. A requirement might say that they should be able to make a request anonymously.

That could be useful. It could also be too quick a conclusion. Some of the people may want to share their reasons with one particular person rather than with the whole group. Others might want to participate in a system in which certain reasons can be considered openly. The design depends on what they regard as a legitimate way to decide who gets access.

An account of the practice can preserve these different positions. A list of requirements may need to select among them or establish a way for them to coexist. The list is therefore a result of a further design decision. We should be able to tell the difference between what the inquiry established and the choice a designer made on the basis of it.

That distinction is helpful when evaluating a paper. A finding can be analytically interesting even if I disagree with the design recommendation that follows from it. Perhaps the authors have identified an important feature of a practice and chosen one way to respond. Other researchers may develop a different response that follows from the same understanding.

We can ask whether a design recommendation follows from the analysis, and also whether it requires additional assumptions. A finding that workshop members value discretion might support private requests, negotiated exchanges, or leaving the existing arrangement alone. Deciding among those responses requires a further account of the consequences of each design.

If we require the findings to justify one particular system, we can make it difficult to preserve that possibility. The inquiry may become a story about why the proposed features were necessary, and the parts of people's lives that suggest another interpretation may become harder to see.

## What changes when implementing the features becomes cheap?

A team can already use language models to help summarize interviews and develop candidate designs. As the ability to generate software improves, it will be increasingly feasible to turn those designs into working prototypes. We could gather many proposed requirements, produce several versions of a system, and investigate how well they perform.

I welcome much of this capability. It could give small research teams a way to investigate design possibilities they previously could not afford to realize. It could also help people affected by a system examine more alternatives before deciding how the system should work.

At the same time, a quick movement from an account of people's lives to a working design can conceal a misunderstanding. The scheduling system might be remarkably easy to use while changing a relationship that mattered to the members of the workshop. A set of positive usability results may give the project little reason to return to its original interpretation of the practice.

This is an important difference between producing a solution and developing an account through which a situation becomes more intelligible. The second activity can reveal why the first is headed in the wrong direction. It can also show why two solutions that seem to serve the same purpose have different consequences for the people involved.

I expect AI to participate in both activities. It may become very good at analyzing accounts, suggesting interpretations, and helping researchers examine alternative explanations. I do not see the future of qualitative research as a matter of protecting a particular procedure from automation. I see an opportunity to reconsider what kind of knowledge the procedure is trying to produce.

## What a good qualitative finding owes its reader

An ethnographic or qualitative account should be open to criticism. The author needs to explain the circumstances of the inquiry, the observations that led to an interpretation, and the reasons that other interpretations are less useful or remain possible. A claim about a particular group should not quietly become a claim about everyone who performs a similar activity.

We can ask difficult questions about such a contribution without requiring it to produce a list of features. Does the account help us understand something that was previously difficult to recognize? Does it explain how the different parts of a practice are related? Can we see the distinction the authors want us to notice in the material they present? What would make us reconsider their interpretation?

Some work will be primarily concerned with developing a design. In that setting, it is reasonable to examine how the design follows from the investigation and what happens when people use it. Other work can have a different purpose. A study may make it possible for several researchers to investigate different aspects of the same phenomenon.

When implementation becomes inexpensive, a field can pursue a great many responses to the same qualitative finding. Some researchers may develop a new scheduling system. Others may reconsider the rules of the workshop or decide to leave an existing arrangement alone. The account has scientific value if it helps these people understand the practice more precisely, including why their decisions differ.

A study of someone's life can give us a better question than the one through which we originally approached them. We should recognize that contribution before requiring the person to become a set of requirements for a system.
