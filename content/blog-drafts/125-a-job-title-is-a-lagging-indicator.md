# A Job Title Is a Lagging Indicator

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** O. Careers, institutions, and the economics of expertise | **Candidate tags:** career-advice, sociology-of-science

Two people describe themselves as software engineers. One spends much of their day developing a new product. The other investigates problems in a service that has been operating for years. A third person with the same title may coordinate decisions across several teams. The title tells us something about the profession, but relatively little about what these people do on a Tuesday afternoon.

This matters when we ask which professions AI will change. A useful starting point is to examine the activities that currently give a role its value. Some may become much easier to perform. Others may be combined into a new arrangement. The institution may retain the old title while changing the work that belongs to it.

The opposite can also happen. A new job title can describe a familiar kind of work carried out with different tools. Its appearance may tell us more about how organizations are trying to present their activities than about a fundamentally new form of expertise.

I expect many of the important changes to occur before they become visible in the names of occupations. People may be asked to take on different responsibilities or learn new ways of contributing while continuing to occupy the same positions. The availability of new tools can also change the activities through which beginners enter a profession.

For someone trying to understand a changing field, it is useful to look underneath the title. Which things do people need to be able to do? Which decisions do institutions rely on them to make? What conditions allow them to become useful in those roles? These questions may tell us more than a list of occupations expected to survive.
