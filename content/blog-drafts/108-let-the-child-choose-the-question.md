# Let the Child Choose the Question

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** N. Learning, teaching, and shared practice | **Candidate tags:** education, participatory-design

A child in a making activity wants to build a moving bird. The teacher could provide a set of steps that leads to a working model. The child might learn a great deal through following them. Another possibility is to let the child decide what the bird should do.

The second activity creates a different kind of problem. The child may imagine a movement they cannot yet build. They may need to examine several mechanisms, change the shape of the bird, or revise the movement itself. A teacher can introduce new knowledge at the moments when that knowledge helps the child understand their choices.

This is one of the attractions of constructionist approaches to learning. Making something meaningful can give a learner a reason to investigate a difficult idea. The thing being made can also become an object through which a teacher and learner discuss their understanding.

Greater choice does not guarantee learning. A project can become so difficult that the child spends most of the time waiting for help. A familiar activity can be a better starting point when a learner has little experience with the material. The teacher's work includes deciding what kinds of questions the available tools and time can support.

AI and computational design could make more of a child's questions feasible to pursue. I hope educational research will examine how the teacher's guidance changes under those conditions. When technical support allows a learner to choose what to investigate, the important educational work may involve helping them develop a question that they can continue to make their own.

## Editorial source notes

Reading leads: Seymour Papert, Mindstorms (1980); constructionism, project-based learning, and guided inquiry. The bird activity is hypothetical. Distinguish choosing a goal, conducting an inquiry, and acquiring a specified concept.
