# The Argument That Changed My Mind

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** P. Knowledge, meaning, and life after common measures | **Candidate tags:** humility, philosophy-of-science

The most useful criticism of a belief sometimes helps me understand the condition I had overlooked when forming it. I may retain much of the evidence and reasoning that led to my position, while reconsidering a relation between them. That change can be more interesting than deciding who had won the argument.

Imagine two researchers disagreeing about an interface. One argues that it gives users more freedom. The other worries that it makes people responsible for decisions they do not understand. Each may have a good account of the part of the experience they are considering. Their disagreement can help them discover that they were using freedom to describe two different relationships.

If one researcher changes their mind, it need not mean that they accept every part of the other's account. They may recognize a condition missing from their original explanation. They may leave the discussion with a more specific belief and a clearer way to investigate it.

The disagreement can become more useful if they identify a situation in which their accounts lead to different judgments. A person might be able to choose among many operations while having little understanding of their consequences. Examining that situation can help both researchers see why their original claims appeared to conflict, even if they still disagree about the appropriate design.

I find this a valuable way to think about criticism. The purpose of a good argument is partly to expose the assumptions connecting observations to conclusions. A person can reconsider one assumption without having to reject every part of the work that depended on it.

As AI makes persuasive arguments inexpensive, we may encounter more well-formed reasons to accept or reject almost any position. It becomes important to know what an argument has actually changed in our understanding. Which observation did we overlook? Which relationship had we misunderstood? Which alternative would we now examine?

A productive disagreement can leave both people with a more specific account of what they believe and why. They may discover that they were using the same term to protect different purposes, or that an assumption explains their different judgments. The result worth carrying forward is the part of the world that has become clearer through the argument.
