# When Two Papers Appear to Disagree

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** M. Research as a practice | **Candidate tags:** philosophy-of-science, academia

Two studies of the same interface can report different results because the people in the studies are doing different kinds of work. If an approach helps experienced practitioners and frustrates beginners, we may have an opportunity to understand the practice more precisely. The difference in the results has become a question about the conditions under which the interface is useful.

Sometimes the useful contribution of reading both papers is a better question. Perhaps the method is useful when a person already understands the structure of the task, and less useful while they are learning that structure. The apparent disagreement has identified a condition under which the effect might change.

This is not a reason to explain away every inconsistent result. Researchers can make errors, and studies can genuinely contradict one another. It is important to distinguish a possible explanation of a discrepancy from evidence that the explanation is correct. A subsequent study might need to vary the condition that we believe accounts for the difference.

The next investigation could compare experienced and novice users under a common task while examining how each understands the available operations. If the predicted difference does not appear, the expertise-based explanation deserves reconsideration. This makes a possible reconciliation of two papers into a question that can be investigated.

As research becomes more abundant, we may have many accounts of similar methods in different settings. An agent can summarize those accounts and identify contradictory sentences. The more demanding work is to recover the relation between each claim and the conditions under which its evidence was obtained.

A useful literature review should let a reader see which conditions might account for a disagreement and where a real contradiction remains. Sometimes that will lead to a new experiment. Sometimes it will give us a better explanation of why two defensible pieces of evidence belong to different questions. That is scientific progress made through reading.

## Editorial source notes
