# The Same Thing Done Twice

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** P. Knowledge, meaning, and life after common measures | **Candidate tags:** philosophy-of-science, ethics

Imagine receiving two small wooden objects that look exactly alike. One was made by a machine at the request of a friend. The friend made the other by hand. Some people would attach different meanings to the two gifts even if they could find no difference in their appearance.

The meaning in this example belongs partly to the activity through which the object came to exist. Making the object may have required the friend's time and attention. The machine-made object could have required careful thought of a different kind. We might value either one for reasons that the resulting shape does not reveal.

This is a useful distinction in an age when the quality of many products may become easier to reproduce. If a painting, a piece of writing, or an object can be reproduced with great fidelity, its observable properties may tell us relatively little about the relationship through which it was made. The history of production can become an independent part of what people care about.

There are difficult questions here. Some people may place too much value on a particular story about production. Stories can be false, and a genuine history of effort does not necessarily make an object worth more to another person. We need a way to distinguish the provenance of an artifact from the value a person assigns to that provenance.

I expect more activities to reveal this distinction as machine production becomes capable of realizing the same outcomes through different processes. The value of an object may be inseparable from the way a particular person encountered it, received it, or participated in its creation. Two artifacts can share a representation without occupying the same place in someone's life.

## Editorial source notes

Reading leads: Walter Benjamin on mechanical reproduction, theories of authenticity and provenance, and studies of craft and gift exchange. This is a hypothetical case; avoid implying a universal preference for human-made objects.
