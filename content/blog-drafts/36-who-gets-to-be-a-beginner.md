# Who Gets to Be a Beginner?

**Status:** Long-form editorial manuscript | **Theme:** Education and the next generation of expertise | **Candidate tags:** education, career-advice, sociology-of-science

A profession can continue to need experts while losing the work through which people used to become experts. This is a risk I find particularly important as AI becomes capable of performing much of the work given to beginners.

An engineer might begin by fixing a small problem in a larger system. In doing so, they encounter the decisions of other engineers, receive guidance, and gradually develop the ability to take responsibility for more difficult work. The organization obtains a useful result, and the engineer receives an opportunity to learn. The same activity helps finance both. When the organization can obtain the result without employing a beginner, those two purposes may need to be organized separately.

## The work through which a beginner learns

Suppose a person joins a software team. They are given a relatively small task in a system that has been developed over many years. The task is useful because something in the system needs to change. It also gives the person a reason to understand the code, the tests, and the circumstances in which the change will be used.

They might receive comments from a more experienced colleague. The colleague points out something that a tutorial on the programming language would have had little reason to explain: another component relies on the current behavior, and changing it could affect people elsewhere in the organization. The new engineer learns something about the system through being responsible for a small part of it.

There are similar arrangements in other fields. Someone preparing equipment for an experiment may learn how the equipment is used. A designer working on a small component may encounter the larger practice in which the component will be used. A person doing a relatively simple task can acquire knowledge about the more complicated work surrounding it.

Of course, much entry-level work is a poor educational experience. A beginner can spend a great deal of time performing a repetitive task without acquiring a useful understanding of the field. The fact that someone receives payment for an activity does not mean the activity is a good way to learn.

The useful arrangement is the one in which work brings a beginner into contact with new problems, more experienced people, and the consequences of their own decisions. Collins and Kapur discuss some of the educational mechanisms involved in guided participation in their chapter on [cognitive apprenticeship](https://doi.org/10.1017/9781108888295.010). They consider how people can develop conceptual knowledge through situated activity, guidance, and reflection. The model helps explain why giving someone the answer to a small problem is different from helping them learn through participating in a practice.

## When the work is no longer worth paying for

Now imagine that the small software task can be completed by an agent in a few minutes. The agent can investigate the code, propose a change, and run the tests. An experienced engineer can review the result.

There is a compelling reason for the organization to use that arrangement. It may obtain a better result with less effort. There may be many other things that its experienced engineers can investigate now that some of the smaller tasks have become inexpensive.

The change also removes an occasion for a person to enter the practice. The organization could continue hiring beginners and give them different opportunities to develop the relevant knowledge. It may decide that doing so is important. The economic reason for asking them to perform the old task has become weaker.

An individual firm might reasonably rely on experienced hires rather than pay for training whose benefits could eventually accrue to another employer. If many firms make the same decision, however, the supply of experienced people can become harder to replenish. That possibility is a reason to examine the arrangements that distribute the cost of apprenticeship across institutions. It is a mechanism to investigate, rather than a consequence established by the employment evidence alone.

I find the distinction between the demand for labor and the reproduction of expertise particularly important here. An organization can reduce its demand for the activities that beginners perform without reducing its desire to work with experienced people. In the short term, it can draw on a supply of people who learned under earlier arrangements.

The consequences of that decision might become more apparent several years later. If other organizations have made similar choices, there could be fewer people with experience of the work that remains difficult or consequential. A field might expect someone to arrive with an understanding that it provides fewer opportunities to acquire.

This is a possible mechanism, rather than a forecast we can establish by examining the capability of a particular model. The organization can develop new forms of training. New technologies can also create work that offers beginners a useful way into the profession. We need to examine whether those arrangements actually develop.

There is already some evidence that gives us a reason to investigate the question. In the August 2026 revision of [Canaries in the Coal Mine?](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/app/uploads/2026/08/Canaries_August2026.pdf), Brynjolfsson, Chandar, and Chen report a widening relative employment gap among U.S. workers aged 22 to 25 in occupations more exposed to AI. They find that differences in hiring contribute substantially to the pattern. They also report no evidence of widespread economy-wide displacement in the data they analyze.

The authors treat their findings as descriptive rather than causal. They note qualifications involving prior trends, education, and differences among data sources. I would not use the paper to claim that AI has eliminated a particular percentage of junior software jobs. It is useful because it makes the distribution of opportunities to enter occupations an empirical question that we can begin to examine.

## Can an agent provide the apprenticeship?

A capable assistant can offer a beginner many kinds of help that were previously difficult to obtain. Someone can ask how a complicated system works, request examples, and explore alternative implementations. They can receive feedback at a moment when an experienced colleague is unavailable.

I expect those capabilities to make it much easier to develop some forms of expertise outside a conventional workplace. They might also improve the quality of the learning that occurs within one. A beginner could use an agent to investigate more possibilities, while a colleague helps them understand which possibilities matter to the actual work.

There is another part of apprenticeship that deserves attention. A person needs to encounter conditions under which their knowledge is going to be used. They may need permission to examine an unfamiliar system, observe the consequences of a decision, or take responsibility for something that matters to other people.

An assistant can simulate many of those conditions. It can also help organize a real opportunity to participate. Whether an institution gives a beginner access to that opportunity is a different question. A person can have excellent instruction available and little access to the people, equipment, or decisions through which they would develop a particular professional understanding.

This is an important distinction for thinking about the claim that education will become inexpensive. The instructional component may become remarkably accessible. We may still need arrangements that give people time to learn, provide relevant experiences, and allow them to develop relationships within a practice.

## Who carries the cost?

A person learning while doing useful work can receive payment for some of the time it takes to become knowledgeable. If the work no longer needs to be done by a person, learning may become an activity they are expected to finance separately.

One possible response is for organizations to establish deliberate apprenticeship programs, in which beginners contribute to useful projects while receiving guidance. Another is for universities and other educational institutions to give people access to richer practical experiences. Communities might maintain shared environments in which people can investigate real problems and learn from one another.

All of those arrangements require resources. The distribution of those resources will influence who gets to develop expertise. A person who can afford to spend a year learning without receiving an income has different opportunities from someone who needs to support themselves while they learn.

I expect this difference to become more important if many conventional entry-level activities disappear. The ability to learn may become much more widely available in one sense, while the opportunity to establish oneself within a profession becomes more dependent on the circumstances of one's life.

The prediction should be tested through the routes people actually take into a profession. I would look at whether new forms of paid work, mentorship, and shared learning give a broad population useful opportunities to develop expertise. If those arrangements emerge, the loss of a particular entry-level task need not leave a lasting gap.

The broader concern is who can afford the time and conditions required to become knowledgeable. A society can give almost everyone access to a remarkable tutor and reserve the experience of becoming a professional for people who can finance it themselves. Preventing that outcome requires an arrangement for learning, rather than another assurance about the future value of expertise.
