# The Work a Definition Has to Do

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** G. Writing, explanation, and representation | **Candidate tags:** philosophy-of-science, computer-science

Consider a paper that describes a robot as autonomous. A reader might imagine a robot that can decide what to do in a new setting. The authors may mean that it can carry out a specified task without continuous human control. Both are reasonable uses of the word. They make different claims.

A definition helps when it tells us what we should expect to observe. If autonomy means that a robot can finish a task without an operator, we can examine when assistance is required. If it includes the ability to reconsider the task, we need evidence of another kind. A shared word does little to resolve this difference unless the paper also explains the behavior it refers to.

This is one reason I prefer definitions built around examples and consequences. A definition of a design space becomes clearer when we can see a design that belongs to it and one that does not. A definition of a usable mechanism becomes clearer when we know which changes would make the mechanism unusable. The boundary gives the term a purpose.

There are cases where established terminology should be preserved. Replacing precise technical terms with everyday synonyms can make an argument less accurate. The challenge is to introduce the term when the reader has enough of the underlying idea to recognize it.

Scientific disagreement often becomes more productive once we know which claim we are disagreeing about. A definition should help us reach that point. I am less interested in whether two researchers use exactly the same word than in whether they can tell what each other's word commits them to.
