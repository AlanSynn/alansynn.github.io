# The Hidden Work of Being Easy to Use

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** K. Interaction, delegation, and responsibility | **Candidate tags:** participatory-design, sociology-of-science

An interface that makes a teacher's work easier may rely on the work of someone who never appears in a usability study. For example, an inventory system can make ordering supplies straightforward because another person continually corrects its records and handles unusual requests. The improvement is real, but its cost belongs to a larger arrangement.

Consider a school that uses a digital tool for managing supplies. A teacher may be able to order materials with a few clicks. Another member of staff may spend hours correcting inventory records and resolving exceptions that the interface cannot express. The experience of the teacher has genuinely improved. The total arrangement may still require substantial work.

The activity extends beyond the person sitting in front of the screen. In an organization, removing a step from one person's workflow can introduce a step somewhere else. The work may also move to the people who are least visible in an evaluation.

Automation makes this easy to miss. A system may respond well because staff continually clean its data, check its decisions, or solve the cases it cannot handle. Those people are part of the system through which the apparent ease is achieved.

We might follow a particular request from the moment it enters the interface until the underlying work is complete. If the visible task becomes shorter while an exception creates new work for another person, the two changes should appear in the account. A total time saving may still be worthwhile; we also need to understand where the remaining responsibility has moved.

A useful account of usability should be able to follow a request through the people and systems that make it possible. We may discover that an automation removed substantial work, concentrated it in a more appropriate place, or made another person's responsibilities harder to sustain. Those are different outcomes, and a study of the visible interface may give us no way to distinguish them.

## Editorial source notes

Reading leads: Star and Strauss on visible and invisible work, CSCW, and studies of data work. Develop one traced workflow with evidence of shifted labor; avoid assuming every usability improvement merely redistributes work.
