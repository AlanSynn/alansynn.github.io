# When Every Paper Sounds the Same

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** G. Writing, explanation, and representation | **Candidate tags:** academia, sociology-of-science

A theory paper and an account of a participatory design study can both be clear while sounding quite different. Each field has developed ways of writing that help readers recognize the kind of evidence being offered. I worry that some of those differences may become less visible when an AI editor gives every paper the same kind of polished prose.

Research prose carries information about how a field reasons. The words used to describe a proof, the language used to report an observation of a participant, and the terms used to characterize a computational workload give readers expectations about what has been established. A familiar writing convention can help a community recognize the type of claim being made.

For example, a mathematical claim can be false because one allowed case violates a stated guarantee. An account of a participant's activity can remain useful without representing every possible experience. Editing both into language about an approach being generally effective could obscure the distinction between the propositions being supported.

A language model that makes papers sound consistently fluent could improve access for authors and readers. It could also favor a narrow set of rhetorical patterns. A tentative interpretation might become a confident conclusion. A particular observation might be turned into a generalized design principle. Even if none of the reported facts changes, the relation between the evidence and the claim can change.

The concern is what a style permits us to say, including the kinds of uncertainty we can express. Editing should preserve the kinds of uncertainty, specificity, and reasoning that matter to the work. Two authors can write clearly and sound quite different.

What I would preserve through editing is the relation between the form of the explanation and the kind of knowledge the author wants to share. Shared conventions can make a paper easier to read. They should also leave room for a particular discipline, and sometimes a particular researcher, to describe a problem in a way that reveals something about how it was understood.
