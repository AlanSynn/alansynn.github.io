# Confidence Has an Address

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** P. Knowledge, meaning, and life after common measures | **Candidate tags:** uncertainty, philosophy-of-science

I can be quite confident in a prediction about automated program optimization and much less confident in a prediction about what will happen to the institutions that employ programmers. These judgments rely on different kinds of evidence and different conditions. Treating them as one general confidence about AI conceals the distinction.

A claim about a technology often expands into several claims about the world in which it will be used. A researcher may say they are confident that robots will become much more capable, and then appear to offer a similarly confident view of what will happen to employment. The two predictions involve different conditions and different kinds of evidence.

A useful expression of confidence identifies the claim to which it belongs. I can expect substantial progress in automated optimization while remaining unsure how quickly institutions will change their ways of recognizing research contributions. One forecast may have a relatively clear mechanism. The other depends partly on choices people and organizations have yet to make.

This matters in evaluating other people's arguments as well. A person who is confident about an observed phenomenon may be much less certain about its explanation. Someone who is confident about an explanation under a stated set of assumptions may have little basis for applying it to another setting. We learn more from locating the uncertainty than from giving the entire account one level of confidence.

It is also possible to have different reasons for revising related predictions. A new technical capability might change the expected speed of automation, while evidence about hiring practices changes the expected institutional response. Specifying these dependencies allows us to update the relevant part of the argument without pretending that every kind of evidence bears on every conclusion.

A stronger prediction can make these locations visible. It can tell us which observations would affect which part of the forecast. Changing our mind about one condition then need not require either defending or abandoning the whole prediction.

A useful forecast identifies the parts of its account that a new observation could change. The capabilities of a system may develop as predicted while the institutional response goes in another direction. Being precise about where confidence belongs makes it possible to learn from the second result without pretending that either the entire forecast succeeded or the entire forecast failed.
