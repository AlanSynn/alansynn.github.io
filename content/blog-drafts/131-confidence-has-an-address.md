# Confidence Has an Address

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** P. Knowledge, meaning, and life after common measures | **Candidate tags:** uncertainty, philosophy-of-science

I can be confident that a mechanism moves the way I observed it this morning and uncertain that it will survive a year of use. The difference lies in what the confidence is about.

It is often tempting to describe ourselves as generally confident or uncertain about a technology. A researcher may say they are confident that robots will become much more capable, and then appear to offer a similarly confident view of what will happen to employment. The two predictions involve different conditions and different kinds of evidence.

A useful expression of confidence identifies the claim to which it belongs. I can expect substantial progress in automated optimization while remaining unsure how quickly institutions will change their ways of recognizing research contributions. One forecast may have a relatively clear mechanism. The other depends partly on choices people and organizations have yet to make.

This matters in evaluating other people's arguments as well. A person who is confident about an observed phenomenon may be much less certain about its explanation. Someone who is confident about an explanation under a stated set of assumptions may have little basis for applying it to another setting. We learn more from locating the uncertainty than from giving the entire account one level of confidence.

A stronger prediction can make these locations visible. It can tell us which observations would affect which part of the forecast. Changing our mind about one condition then need not require either defending or abandoning the whole prediction.

I think this is an especially useful habit when the future is changing quickly. Confidence should belong to a claim and the conditions under which we have reason to make it.
