# The Picture That Would Have Been Wrong from Another Seat

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** H. Graphics, vision, and perception | **Candidate tags:** computer-science, philosophy-of-science

A drawing on a pavement can look like a deep hole when photographed from a particular position. Walk a few steps to the side and the illusion changes. The image was constructed for a relationship between the drawing and the person looking at it.

I like this example when thinking about visual systems that are evaluated from a fixed viewing position. The quality of a depiction can depend on where a viewer stands, how they move, and what their visual system uses to interpret the scene. Some images work because they reproduce a particular projection. Others work because the objects remain coherent as the viewer explores them.

Virtual and augmented reality bring this relationship into the system itself. When a person moves their head, the displayed image has to change in a way that supports the intended spatial experience. An accurate frame considered in isolation can be part of a poor experience if it arrives at the wrong time or responds incorrectly to motion.

The relevant evidence is therefore often a trajectory of observations rather than an individual image. We need to see how the representation behaves while someone changes position. A static comparison may be appropriate for a static task. It cannot establish all the properties of an interactive visual medium.

This is an example of a broader opportunity in graphics. The display, the world, and the viewer form a system. Studying the connections among them can reveal research questions that never appear when we treat the generated image as the final object of evaluation.
