# A Mechanism You Can Repair

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** I. Craft, mechanisms, and fabrication | **Candidate tags:** computer-science, participatory-design

Counting the parts of a mechanism can tell us something about its construction. A three-part mechanism may nevertheless be difficult for its owner to repair if one of the parts requires specialized equipment. A five-part mechanism made from familiar components may give that person a much simpler way to keep the object working.

These two mechanisms give us several reasons to call a construction simple. It is worth asking what simplicity allows someone to do. A mechanism may be simple to fabricate, simple to assemble, simple to understand, or simple to repair. These properties often travel together, but they need not do so.

The distinction matters when a design system generates mechanisms. It can count components and estimate the dimensions of each. Those are useful properties for comparing candidates. If the person will be constructing the object by hand, the relationship between the components and the maker's available tools may matter more. A mechanism that requires careful alignment once may be easier to maintain than one requiring repeated adjustment.

A repairable design has to be understood relative to the person who will maintain it. A sealed assembly may be more reliable in ordinary operation, while a collection of familiar parts may be easier to repair after a particular failure. Comparing the designs requires us to consider both the kinds of failures likely to occur and the resources available when they do.

Consider an automaton in which a wooden piece wears out after repeated use. A maker who understands how the piece participates in the motion can inspect the problem and make a replacement. A more compact mechanism could make that process much harder. The first design has given the maker a capability that remains after the software is gone.

For an automated design system, the useful definition of simplicity should follow the operations a maker needs to perform. A design can reduce the number of components while increasing the expertise or equipment required for a later repair. A mechanism that a person can understand, build, and restore provides a different kind of simplicity from a mechanism that uses the fewest parts.
