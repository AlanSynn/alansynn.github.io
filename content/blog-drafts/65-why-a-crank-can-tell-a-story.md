# Why a Crank Can Tell a Story

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** I. Craft, mechanisms, and fabrication | **Candidate tags:** computer-science, participatory-design

Turn the crank of an automaton. A figure lifts its hand, pauses, and lowers it. Another part begins moving at a different point in the rotation. The maker has used a single mechanical input to arrange several events in a sequence that a viewer can interpret as a story.

The angle of the crank supplies a common phase for the movements. A mechanism can arrange one part to rise before another begins to move. If the person turns the crank at different speeds, the relative order of those events can remain stable while their timing in seconds changes. That distinction matters when an expressive effect depends on a pause of a particular duration.

Someone designing a mechanism for a specific trajectory may want to minimize the error between its movement and a target curve. A storyteller may care more about the relation between two moments. The hand needs to rise before a door opens; the figure needs to pause long enough for the viewer to notice the expression. The technical and expressive constraints meet in the same object.

A design tool can help by making these relationships visible and adjustable. It can also help a maker understand which timing relationships require a different mechanism, and which can be changed within the current one. More automation in mechanical synthesis could give people much greater access to this form of expression.

The constraint of a single crank gives the maker a particular medium for composing events. Some sequences will be difficult or impossible to realize mechanically. Within the possible sequences, a difference in phase or in the shape of a cam may determine when the viewer recognizes one movement as a response to another. That relationship belongs to the expressive design of the mechanism.
