# The Difference Between Cheap and Free

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** O. Careers, institutions, and the economics of expertise | **Candidate tags:** sociology-of-science, ethics

A student can download a powerful program at no cost. They still need a computer that can run it, time to understand how to use it, and somewhere to do the work. Someone else may have all of those things already. The price of the program tells us only part of the cost of participating in the activity.

The same distinction applies to intelligence delivered through software. Making a capability inexpensive can open important opportunities. But the capability may be useful only when combined with equipment, knowledge, permission, or access to other people. These complementary conditions can become more consequential as the capability itself becomes abundant.

Imagine a tool that helps anyone design a complicated object. A person with access to a workshop can try several versions and learn from their failures. A person without that access may produce an excellent design and never find out how it behaves when built. The two people have received the same computational assistance. Their opportunities to investigate the resulting possibilities remain different.

There is also a question of continuity. A service that is inexpensive today may become difficult to obtain tomorrow. Someone who has reorganized their work around it may bear a substantial cost in moving elsewhere.

I expect access to powerful computational tools to improve many lives. I would like us to study access in terms of the activities people can actually undertake and sustain. A falling price is an important change, but it is the beginning of that investigation rather than its conclusion.
