# Learning to Ask for Help

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** J. Robotics, control, and situated intelligence | **Candidate tags:** robotics, participatory-design, ai

A robot that asks for help at every unfamiliar situation will become tiring to use. A robot that never asks may quietly continue under an incorrect assumption. Both machines can have substantial technical capabilities.

Suppose a robot is preparing a table and encounters a container it cannot open. A useful request for help would tell the person what the robot has already tried, where it is uncertain, and what small action could allow the task to continue. A general announcement of failure gives the person the additional work of diagnosing the problem.

There are several kinds of help a robot might need. It can ask for missing information, request physical assistance, seek permission, or invite a person to choose among several outcomes. Treating these as one category obscures the responsibility that a request transfers to the user.

More capable robots will presumably need less assistance in many tasks. The requests that remain may become more particular to the setting. A system could be very good at sensing and manipulation yet need to ask who is allowed to use an object in a shared environment.

The important question for HRI may be how to make asking for help part of an ordinary working relationship. Good requests should be specific, arrive at a useful moment, and leave people free to decline when possible. A system that knows when another person has the information or authority it lacks can be more dependable than one that simply acts with greater confidence.
