# The Right to Remain Unmeasured

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** P. Knowledge, meaning, and life after common measures | **Candidate tags:** ethics, education, participatory-design

A person learning to draw may want to show their work to a friend without being evaluated. They may want to experiment with a style without keeping a record of how much they have improved. The activity can be meaningful even if nobody has measured its progress.

Technology makes it easier to record and evaluate many aspects of an activity. A learning platform can retain a detailed account of a student's attempts. A creative tool can estimate how well an artifact matches some established criteria. These capabilities can provide useful feedback, especially when someone wants help understanding a particular difficulty.

The availability of an evaluation does not establish that the person wants the activity to become a source of evidence about them. A record can change the relationship people have with their own attempts. They may become concerned about what a future observer could infer from a mistake that was originally part of an ordinary experiment.

I find it useful to distinguish feedback someone seeks from evaluation imposed by an institution. An educational program may have good reasons to assess learning. A private exploration can have different purposes. The people involved should understand what is being recorded, what decisions the record might inform, and which parts of their activities can remain outside that arrangement.

As automatic evaluation becomes more capable, I expect the choice of when to evaluate to matter increasingly. We can benefit from tools that help us understand what we are doing without asking every activity to provide a continuous account of our abilities. Some experiences are worth having without being turned into evidence of progress.
