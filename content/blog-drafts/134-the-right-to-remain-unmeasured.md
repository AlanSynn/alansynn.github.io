# The Right to Remain Unmeasured

**Status:** Long-form editorial manuscript | **Theme:** P. Knowledge, meaning, and life after common measures | **Candidate tags:** ethics, education, participatory-design

There are activities I would like people to be able to pursue without having their participation converted into an assessment of them. A person can learn to draw, make something for a friend, or spend an afternoon trying out an unfamiliar style. An accurate evaluation of what they produced may be useful to them. Whether the activity should create a record of their ability is a separate question.

A drawing tool could retain every attempt, estimate its quality, and offer an account of the person's progress. The person may welcome that information when they want to understand a particular difficulty. If the record can later be used to evaluate them for another purpose, an attempt that would otherwise have belonged to a private afternoon acquires a different consequence.

The ability to measure more things gives us greater reason to think about when measurement is legitimate.

## What a measure is for

Consider a learner who wants to improve at drawing a particular shape. They can compare several attempts, examine the differences, and ask someone to help them understand a difficulty. A system that measures aspects of the drawing could give them useful feedback.

Suppose the same learner is interested in seeing what happens when they deliberately draw the shape incorrectly. Perhaps a familiar object becomes interesting when its proportions change. An evaluation designed to estimate accuracy could describe the departure from the original shape perfectly well. It might say very little about whether the experiment was worthwhile.

The measurement is useful in relation to a purpose. The learner may want to recover the ability to draw a particular form, or they may want to create a different form. They could move between these purposes during a single afternoon.

In [Measurement and Fairness](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/measurement-and-fairness/), Abigail Z. Jacobs and Hanna Wallach examine how measurement and the validity of measurements matter to questions of fairness in computational systems. I find their attention to the relationship between an unobservable or complex construct and the way we measure it useful beyond fairness evaluation. We should understand the property we are trying to investigate and what a measurement can establish about it.

That is one question. Another concerns the reason we have for measuring the property in a particular person's activity.

We may be able to construct a reasonable measure of a learner's accuracy. If the learner is experimenting with a drawing because they find the activity enjoyable, it may be of little importance to measure their accuracy at that moment. The capacity to produce a valid measurement does not by itself determine whether producing the measurement is desirable.

## Feedback that belongs to a person

Imagine two people using the same educational tool. The first asks the tool to help identify a difficulty in their work. They want to compare their attempts and develop an account of their progress. The second uses the tool to make something for a friend. They are interested in finishing the object and have little reason to evaluate their learning.

The system could offer both people exactly the same measurement. It would play a different role in their activities.

For the first person, the feedback can become something they choose to investigate. They may ask for a different account of the result or decide to work on another property. They have a reason to welcome the evaluation because it helps them pursue an intention.

The second person may find it distracting. They might be concerned about the purpose for which the resulting assessment will be retained, especially if the tool is part of an educational or professional institution. An evaluation that appears useful to the designer could introduce another obligation for the person using it.

There is a meaningful difference between asking for information about an activity and being subjected to an ongoing assessment through that activity. The second arrangement involves decisions about who may interpret the evidence, what it can be used to decide, and whether the person can challenge the interpretation.

The same distinction applies when an evaluation was originally requested by the person being evaluated. A learner might ask for feedback on a draft and welcome a detailed account of its weaknesses. Using that account later to make an unrelated selection decision would require a separate justification. The appropriateness of the measurement can change when its purpose and audience change.

A student can benefit from detailed feedback while also having reasons to resist a permanent record of every unsuccessful attempt. A person practicing a musical instrument can want help with a difficult passage without wanting to maintain a public representation of their proficiency.

One design possibility is to make feedback more responsive to these distinctions. Let a person choose what they want to investigate and make clear what information will be retained. When assessment is necessary for an institutional purpose, describe that purpose and the conditions under which the information will be used.

The important property of the tool is the arrangement it creates around measurement, rather than the accuracy of the evaluation alone.

## An experiment without a future audience

There is something I value about an activity in which I can try something without having to explain the result to anybody. I can make a poor design, abandon it, and make another one. I can spend some time developing a skill without deciding whether the skill will become part of my professional identity.

Many creative activities contain such moments. An unfinished object can serve its purpose by helping someone notice a possibility. The person may not want the object to remain available for later inspection. The value of the attempt may belong to the activity through which it was made.

Suppose every attempt enters a record through which other people can evaluate the maker. The person now has another thing to consider when deciding what to try. A result can have a consequence beyond its usefulness within the original activity.

This does not tell us that everyone will become less willing to experiment when an evaluation is possible. Some people enjoy maintaining extensive records, and some kinds of observation can make experimentation more interesting. I would want to investigate the conditions under which a record changes a person's choices, rather than assume a universal response.

The possibility is worth taking seriously because digital tools can make the recording of an activity nearly effortless. Evaluation can be performed afterward, using purposes and techniques that did not exist when the record was made. A person may therefore have difficulty knowing what an ordinary attempt will eventually be used to establish about them.

There are meaningful design questions about which activities should produce enduring records, which records should remain local, and what people can remove or prevent others from using. These questions are relevant even when the measurement itself is extremely accurate.

## Where measurement has a legitimate role

There are circumstances in which people have good reasons to insist on assessment. A person preparing a structure that other people will use needs evidence that the structure is safe. An educational institution may need to establish that someone has developed a particular ability before entrusting them with a consequential responsibility. Shared environments can require records that allow people to examine how a decision was made.

I would not argue for the freedom to avoid all of these obligations. Other people can have a legitimate interest in the evidence an activity produces.

The distinction I am trying to develop concerns the scope of that interest. A safety evaluation should establish relevant properties of the structure. It need not become a general assessment of the person who made it. An educational assessment can establish evidence about a particular capability under particular conditions. It need not extend into every activity in which the learner participates.

There will be disagreements about where the boundaries belong. A teacher may have reasons to retain information that a student would prefer to discard. A researcher may need to keep enough of a record to make a scientific claim inspectable. A group working together may need to understand the history of a decision.

Those disagreements deserve attention because they concern the relationships through which people participate in an activity. We can ask what evidence is necessary, who benefits from having it, who bears the cost of keeping it, and whether a less extensive arrangement would serve the same purpose.

This gives the notion of a right to remain unmeasured a deliberately limited meaning. It is a claim that people should have meaningful spaces in which their activities are not converted into persistent assessments of them. It is compatible with voluntary feedback, scientific evidence, and bounded requirements for accountability.

## A society with abundant evaluations

I expect the technical cost of measuring many activities to become very small. A system can examine a drawing, analyze a conversation, evaluate a piece of code, or produce a description of someone's apparent progress. It may become convenient to retain such information in case it is useful later.

I can imagine considerable benefits. People could receive assistance with problems that previously required access to a specialist. They could understand aspects of their own work that had been difficult to examine. More capable tools might help us replace some poorly justified assessments with better evidence.

I also imagine that people will increasingly have reasons to decide which parts of their lives they want to make available for those purposes. The ability to generate an evaluation creates a new kind of choice about the organization of an activity.

A creative community might develop practices in which people freely share unfinished work without assigning a score to it. A learner might use a tool to investigate a particular difficulty and then decide that the resulting record has served its purpose. Another person might want to maintain a detailed history of everything they have made.

We should be able to make room for each of these practices. When measurement serves a legitimate purpose, its scope and the use of the resulting evidence should be intelligible. When a person simply wants to explore, they should have a meaningful way to do so without establishing a permanent account of their abilities.

The prospect of abundant evaluation makes that freedom more consequential. A person who can produce a remarkably good score may still have excellent reasons to spend an afternoon doing something for which they will never receive one.
