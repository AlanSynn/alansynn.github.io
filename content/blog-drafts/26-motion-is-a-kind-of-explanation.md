# Motion Is a Kind of Explanation

**Status:** editorial draft / argument development | **Theme:** Graphics, fabrication, and making | **Candidate tags:** computer-science, robotics, philosophy-of-science

A small change in timing can alter the explanation a viewer constructs from a movement. Consider two animations of a lever approaching a block. In one, the block moves after contact. In the other, it moves just before contact. Both show a similar collection of positions and motions. They invite different accounts of what caused the block to move.

Animation can make relationships available through the order of events. A pause can suggest that a character is waiting for something. A change in the motion of one part can reveal a constraint imposed by another. Someone watching the movement may recognize these relations without needing a verbal explanation.

This is useful when we design a visualization of a physical process. A perfectly accurate animation can be difficult to interpret if an important displacement is too small or occurs too quickly to notice. Slowing down the relevant part of the movement, or deliberately exaggerating it, can help someone see the mechanism. The resulting explanation should remain clear about which aspects of the motion have been changed for presentation.

For a visualization intended to explain contact, a useful comparison might retain the same objects and endpoints while changing when movement begins. Asking viewers what they believe caused the movement would test the explanatory role of timing. A separate physical calculation would be needed if the intended claim concerned whether the depicted force could actually produce the motion.

A related problem appears when a robot moves near a person. The shape and timing of its approach may help the person understand which object it intends to reach or when they will have an opportunity to take a turn. A successful movement and a legible movement are different design achievements. The second depends on what another person can reasonably infer while the movement is occurring.

Motion can also create a misleading account. An animation that makes the block appear to move because of contact does not establish the forces in an actual physical system. A person may interpret a robot's gesture differently from the designer's expectation. We need evidence appropriate to the explanation we are claiming to provide, including what the people observing the movement actually understand.

The opportunity for graphics research extends beyond generating increasingly plausible sequences. A movement can give us a way to examine a relation that would be difficult to see in a static representation. Understanding which relations it makes intelligible, and to whom, is a research question about the movement itself.
