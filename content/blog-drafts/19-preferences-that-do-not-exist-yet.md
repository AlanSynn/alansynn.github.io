# Preferences That Do Not Exist Yet

**Status:** editorial draft / argument development | **Theme:** HCI, institutions, and value | **Candidate tags:** participatory-design, philosophy-of-science

A person cannot always tell us which interface they prefer. Sometimes the interface must exist before the preference can exist. This creates a limit on the familiar picture of design as discovering requirements that were waiting to be collected.

## Working argument

Imagine offering someone a tool for designing their own furniture. Before seeing an editable prototype, they may say they want the tool to make all the decisions. After changing one part and using the result, they may discover that the experience of making it matters. The new preference is not necessarily a better measurement of an old one. The encounter has changed the range of things they can imagine wanting.

Preference learning is useful when judgments are available and reasonably stable. It may also track changing preferences. But a system that only optimizes against already expressed wishes risks narrowing the space in which new wishes emerge. A design researcher may need to create experiences that let people discover a different form of activity. Some preferences emerge through making, play, disagreement, and repeated use.

This is a reason to treat prototypes as instruments for eliciting and transforming experience, rather than small versions of a pre-specified solution. I expect cheaper production to expand the set of experiences people can try, making research on the emergence of values more consequential. The eventual designs may serve very small communities whose interests were not present in the original requirements.

## Challenge

Preference formation can be manipulated. Offering a prototype is an intervention, not a neutral way to observe a person. Distinguish emerging interests from the effects of novelty, social pressure, and persuasive defaults. The essay should not assume every newly formed preference is desirable.
