# Preferences That Do Not Exist Yet

**Status:** Long-form editorial manuscript | **Theme:** HCI, institutions, and value | **Candidate tags:** participatory-design, philosophy-of-science

A design study can begin by asking people what they want and end by discovering something they could not have wanted in quite the same way at the beginning. Making a possibility available can change the purpose of the activity for which the researcher was trying to discover the requirements.

Consider someone who needs a chair. An assistant can ask about the room, the person's taste, and the amount they want to spend. Now give the person a tool with which they can change the chair's shape and examine how it could be built. They may discover that making the chair is the activity they want to pursue. The earlier conversation was concerned with choosing an object; the new experience has given the person another reason to be interested in it.

## Three different things we learn from a choice

When someone chooses an option, we may be learning about a preference they have had for some time. A person knows that they like a particular kind of chair and recognizes it among several alternatives. An assistant can help them find it.

In another case, the person has a reasonably clear preference but has not yet found a way to express it. They know that a certain shape feels comfortable or that a particular color suits the room. It may help to give them examples through which they can show us the distinction. The preference becomes easier to observe through the choices the tool makes available.

There is a third case. The person discovers something interesting in the course of the activity, and that discovery changes the purpose of the activity itself. Before trying the design tool, they had little reason to imagine themselves making furniture. They can now develop a reason to do so.

These possibilities are difficult to distinguish from the final choice alone. In every case, the person might select the same chair. We would need to understand the path to the choice before deciding what kind of change has occurred.

A useful follow-up would examine what the person does when the tool is unavailable or when another way of making the object becomes possible. Continued interest may help distinguish a passing response to novelty from a change in the activity they want to pursue. It would not establish a permanent preference. The purpose of following the activity is to see how the person's reasons develop.

There is a long history of research questioning the idea that people always carry well-defined preferences into decisions. In [Constructive Consumer Choice Processes](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/25/3/187/1795625), Bettman, Luce, and Payne describe how people construct preferences through strategies that depend on the demands of a decision. Their work concerns consumer choice. I want to extend the question to the ways in which a new technology can give someone a reason to participate in an activity they did not previously consider.

The distinction matters because a system designed to recover preferences can be very successful without creating many opportunities for this third kind of discovery.

## What a prototype gives us

Imagine a group of people discussing an authoring tool before anyone has built it. They might agree that the tool should be easy to use and should let them make interesting things. It would be difficult to determine from that conversation exactly what the useful operations should be.

A prototype changes the conversation. Someone can try an operation and discover that it gives them a new way to look at the thing they are making. Another person can use the same operation and find that it interrupts their work. They have acquired a common object through which to investigate the differences in their experience.

In our published work on [MotionSmith](https://doi.org/10.1145/3772318.3791545), an automata design system, the participating artists often revised their motion ideas while examining simulated movements. This is an example of how an authored representation and the alternatives produced by a system can participate in developing an intention. It does not establish that every preference involved in making a design is newly formed.

The more general point concerns the role of a prototype in research. We can use it to examine a question we already have. We can also use it to create a situation in which people develop questions that would have been difficult to anticipate. The prototype is then part of the inquiry into what matters.

This is one of the attractions of research through design. [William Gaver's 2012 essay](https://researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk/en/publications/what-should-we-expect-from-research-through-design/) takes the production of diverse, conceptually interesting artifacts seriously as a source of design knowledge. A useful artifact can give people a way to encounter a particular possibility, and an account of that encounter can make the possibility more intelligible to others.

A prototype that generates an interesting experience need not be the beginning of a product that everyone would want. Sometimes what we learn is that a particular form of activity makes sense for a small group of people. Another group might use the same technical capability to develop an entirely different practice. Both can give us something worth understanding.

## The system that knows what I like

As models become more capable of learning personal preferences, it is tempting to imagine a system that continually finds the best things for a person. If it knows what I like well enough, it can select an appropriate design, present an appealing image, or arrange a useful learning experience without asking many questions.

I would find such a system very useful in many parts of my life. I can have reasonably stable expectations about a routine activity, and I may have little interest in continually investigating other ways of doing it.

There are also activities in which I want to encounter something that gives me a reason to change my preferences. If I am exploring music, I may want a recommendation that is different from the music I usually listen to. If I am making something, I may want to see a possibility that changes my understanding of the object. What counts as a useful suggestion depends on the kind of activity I am trying to engage in.

An excellent preference predictor can help with that exploration. It may have learned enough about a person to suggest alternatives they would find interesting. It could introduce an unfamiliar representation or provide a tool through which a new preference can develop. The possibility of such assistance is one reason I would be careful about claiming that this kind of work must remain the exclusive domain of human designers.

But the system needs to encounter a distinction that is easy to overlook. Predicting a choice someone would make under familiar conditions is different from arranging an experience through which they may come to value something else. If we use the same evaluation for both, we may reward the system for preserving a preference at the moment when changing it would have been valuable.

## The uncomfortable part of forming a preference

We should be careful about celebrating the ability to change what a person wants. There is a difference between making a new activity available to someone and arranging their environment so that they develop a preference that serves the interests of its designer.

A person may come to prefer a particular service because it has become difficult to participate in an activity without it. They may select a suggested design because other options were costly to investigate. They may become interested in a practice because a prototype gave them a genuine opportunity to understand it. Each case can produce an apparently successful prediction of their later behavior.

To understand what happened, we need to examine the conditions under which the preference developed. What alternatives could the person encounter? Could they stop or reconsider the activity? Did their new choice create an obligation that they had not anticipated? Could someone with a different interest pursue another direction?

There is no simple test that separates every meaningful discovery from every form of manipulation. People develop preferences through encounters with others all the time. Education, culture, friendship, and design participate in that process. The responsibility of a research project is to understand enough of the circumstances to make a specific claim about the experience it supported.

Cheaper tools give design research a particularly interesting opportunity here. Practices that would have been too expensive to investigate can become available to small groups. Some will reveal preferences the researchers could have discovered through better examples. Others may create an activity through which people acquire interests they had little reason to develop before.

The number of people who request an experience before it exists is therefore a poor general test of whether it deserves to be made possible. A design can contribute by giving someone a new way to care about what they do. Understanding when and how that happens should be one of the purposes of design research.
