# Making the Jig Before Making the Thing

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** I. Craft, mechanisms, and fabrication | **Candidate tags:** computer-science, robotics

A craftsperson needs to make several identical holes in a piece of material. Instead of carefully measuring each hole, they build a small guide that positions the drill. The guide does not appear in the finished object. It has nevertheless become an important part of how the object can be made.

A jig represents a kind of knowledge. It embodies a decision about where parts should be placed and how an operation should be constrained. Once the jig exists, someone can perform a difficult operation without reasoning about every detail again. A practical result has been moved into the tools available for producing another result.

This is an interesting way to understand automation in fabrication. We often evaluate a machine by whether it makes the desired object. The machine may be more useful if it can also produce the tools, fixtures, and intermediate arrangements that make a class of objects easier to create. The problem of making a thing can become the problem of designing the process through which many things are made.

Sometimes a jig takes more effort to build than the original task. Its usefulness depends on how often the operation will recur, how much variation needs to be supported, and whether other people can use it. A very specialized jig can be an excellent tool for one practice and a poor general solution.

I expect increasingly capable design agents to explore these intermediate artifacts. The research opportunity is to understand what knowledge becomes reusable when a process is turned into a tool, and what choices remain open to the person who will use it.
