# What Counts as a Successful Handoff?

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** K. Interaction, delegation, and responsibility | **Candidate tags:** participatory-design, sociology-of-science

Someone finishes a task and passes the result to a colleague. The colleague receives the file, opens it, and spends the rest of the afternoon trying to understand what was done. The transfer succeeded in one sense. The work has not yet become something the colleague can continue.

A good handoff carries some understanding of the state of a project. Which decisions have been made? Which conditions are still uncertain? What should the next person avoid changing without further investigation? The answers depend on what the recipient is expected to do, rather than on the amount of information the previous person can provide.

Agents will make it easier to produce records of work. They may also complete substantial portions of projects independently. A person who receives the result may need to reconstruct an unfamiliar process, decide which parts to trust, and take responsibility for its next use. A report that everything passed the tests may not provide what they need.

I think this is a useful setting in which to study collaboration. Rather than evaluate a handoff by the speed of transferring information, examine whether the recipient can perform the next meaningful activity. Can they revise a decision, investigate a problem, and recognize a condition that requires help?

Some work is deliberately disposable and requires little handoff. Other work is supposed to survive changes in personnel and tools. A successful transfer makes the result available in a form suited to that future.

## Editorial source notes

Reading leads: CSCW, articulation work (Anselm Strauss), cooperative work and handover studies, and software incident handoffs. Study an actual transfer between roles. Distinct from 63: focus on the recipient's understanding and responsibility rather than format conversion.
