# What a Degree Is Allowed to Promise

**Status:** editorial draft / argument development | **Theme:** Education and the next generation of expertise | **Candidate tags:** education, academia, sociology-of-science

A degree can establish that someone has completed a course of study, acquired a particular ability, or become eligible for a role. These promises have often been connected, and we sometimes use a degree as evidence of a person's more general competence. I expect the connections among them to become more difficult to justify as common intellectual work becomes increasingly accessible through machines.

Consider a graduate who produces excellent software with an assistant. An employer may want to know whether the resulting software is useful, whether the graduate can recognize a problem in the implementation, or whether they can maintain the system after the available tools change. These are reasonable questions about different abilities. A record of the completed project cannot establish all of them.

A qualification can remain important when it describes a capability that another person has a legitimate reason to rely on. Someone taking responsibility for a consequential professional decision may need to demonstrate that they understand particular conditions and can respond appropriately when those conditions change. The assessment should provide evidence of that understanding under the circumstances in which it will matter.

For example, a person might demonstrate that they can perform a procedure with a particular set of tools and recognize when those tools are insufficient. That can be a meaningful commitment to a future employer or institution. It is quite different from using the result to infer that they would outperform another graduate in any unfamiliar intellectual activity.

A credential also serves institutional purposes. It helps organizations select people when they cannot directly investigate every relevant ability. It can make an opportunity available to someone who lacks established professional relationships. Replacing transparent credentials with informal judgments could transfer more power to people who already control access to a field.

The appropriate response to increasingly capable tools is therefore not simply to invent another general rank. We can ask what a particular educational institution is able to establish about a person, through which activities, and for what future purpose. Different roles may require different forms of evidence.

There will be disagreement about whether someone needs to demonstrate an ability independently of a tool. For some purposes, effective collaboration with a system is the capability that matters. In others, the person needs to understand a result well enough to examine it when assistance is unavailable or mistaken.

I would like a degree to carry a more intelligible account of the activities through which its holder developed and demonstrated useful knowledge. That is a bounded and defensible promise. It gives other people a reason to rely on particular capabilities without asking an educational credential to serve as a complete judgment of the person who earned it.
