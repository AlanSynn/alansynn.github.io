# Access Is Not the Same as Having a Voice

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** K. Interaction, delegation, and responsibility | **Candidate tags:** participatory-design, ethics

A participant can attend a design workshop, speak at length, and have little influence over what the group decides. The invitation to participate may have arrived after the decisions that mattered most to them were made. I would distinguish the ability to enter a design process from the authority to change it.

The distinction concerns two different achievements in making a process accessible. Access can allow someone to use a tool, encounter information, or join a conversation. Influence concerns what can change because of their involvement. Both matter, and an improvement in the first does not establish an improvement in the second.

Imagine a group choosing how to automate a shared workplace. An interface collects each person's feedback. The available questions concern convenience and ease of use. Someone who wants to challenge whether a particular activity should be automated has no appropriate place to express the concern. They have been invited to participate in a narrower decision than the one they care about.

Participatory design can help make such boundaries visible. One question worth asking is which parts of the design are genuinely open to revision. Another is whether the people involved can introduce a question the researchers did not anticipate. There are legitimate constraints on a project, including budgets, safety, and responsibilities to people who are absent. Those constraints should be intelligible to participants.

A workshop might allow participants to change the proposed features while leaving the decision to automate outside its scope. That can be a legitimate arrangement if its limits are explicit. It should not be described as giving participants authority over the entire project. Understanding the scope makes it possible to evaluate what their participation actually changed.

A useful evaluation of participation should make visible which decisions were open to revision and what changed through people's involvement. Constraints on the project may be reasonable and need to be explained. Giving someone a way to express a concern becomes more consequential when there is also a way for that concern to affect the work.
