# Access Is Not the Same as Having a Voice

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** K. Interaction, delegation, and responsibility | **Candidate tags:** participatory-design, ethics

A design workshop can be open to everyone and still give some people very little influence over its result. A participant may be able to attend, speak, and offer suggestions. The important decisions may already have been made before the workshop began.

This is a useful distinction when we talk about making technology accessible. Access can allow someone to use a tool, encounter information, or join a conversation. Influence concerns what can change because of their involvement. Both matter, and an improvement in the first does not establish an improvement in the second.

Imagine a group choosing how to automate a shared workplace. An interface collects each person's feedback. The available questions concern convenience and ease of use. Someone who wants to challenge whether a particular activity should be automated has no appropriate place to express the concern. They have been invited to participate in a narrower decision than the one they care about.

Participatory design can help make such boundaries visible. One question worth asking is which parts of the design are genuinely open to revision. Another is whether the people involved can introduce a question the researchers did not anticipate. There are legitimate constraints on a project, including budgets, safety, and responsibilities to people who are absent. Those constraints should be intelligible to participants.

I would like our evaluations of participation to include the changes people were actually able to bring about. A system can make it easier to speak without giving the speaker much power. It is worth understanding both achievements separately.
