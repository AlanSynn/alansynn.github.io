# What We Know With Our Hands

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** P. Knowledge, meaning, and life after common measures | **Candidate tags:** philosophy-of-science, participatory-design

Someone who knows how to work with wood can show another person how much pressure to apply to a tool. They may have difficulty putting the movement into words. Even a careful description can leave the learner unsure of what the appropriate pressure feels like.

I find this distinction important in thinking about knowledge. We can describe an activity, record it in detail, and observe its consequences. We can also develop an ability through actually performing it. Knowing how to recognize and respond to the relevant conditions may require a different kind of engagement from knowing how to describe them.

This does not mean such abilities cannot be learned by a machine. A robot may acquire a useful understanding of contact through repeated interaction with materials. It may develop an ability its designers cannot readily explain in language. That possibility makes the distinction more interesting. Learning an activity and producing an account of it are different achievements, whether the learner is a person or a machine.

There are good reasons to make practical knowledge explicit. Descriptions can help people enter a practice, avoid mistakes, and compare their experiences. The description can be most useful when it directs the learner's attention toward something they can then experience themselves.

As increasingly capable systems make descriptions and instructions abundant, I expect the forms of knowledge people acquire through situated activity to become easier to distinguish. The knowledge involved in making something may become visible in the way people notice, respond to, and work with the material. We should be careful not to treat the absence of a complete verbal account as an absence of knowledge.
