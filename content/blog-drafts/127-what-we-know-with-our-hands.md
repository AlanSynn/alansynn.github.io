# What We Know With Our Hands

**Status:** English working draft | **Theme:** P. Knowledge, meaning, and life after common measures | **Candidate tags:** philosophy-of-science, participatory-design

A maker can show another person how much pressure to apply to a woodworking tool and still have difficulty explaining the movement in words. The demonstration directs the learner toward something they may only come to recognize through handling the tool. Practical knowledge can be communicated through a relationship among instruction, observation, and action.

The distinction concerns the kind of knowledge someone develops through doing an activity. We can describe an activity, record it in detail, and observe its consequences. We can also develop an ability through actually performing it. Knowing how to recognize and respond to the relevant conditions may require a different kind of engagement from knowing how to describe them.

This does not mean such abilities cannot be learned by a machine. A robot may acquire a useful understanding of contact through repeated interaction with materials. It may develop an ability its designers cannot readily explain in language. That possibility makes the distinction more interesting. Learning an activity and producing an account of it are different achievements, whether the learner is a person or a machine.

There are good reasons to make practical knowledge explicit. Descriptions can help people enter a practice, avoid mistakes, and compare their experiences. The description can be most useful when it directs the learner's attention toward something they can then experience themselves.

An explanation can identify the part of a movement to which a learner should attend. The learner may recognize it for the first time while holding the tool. That recognition can make a previously unhelpful instruction intelligible. The useful relation between language and practical knowledge can run in both directions as a person develops their ability to act.

A good description of a practice can help a learner recognize what they should attend to. The next encounter with the material may provide a distinction that no description alone could give them. Research on tools that support this kind of learning should examine how an explanation and an experience work together, including the occasions when the experience reveals something that changes the explanation.
