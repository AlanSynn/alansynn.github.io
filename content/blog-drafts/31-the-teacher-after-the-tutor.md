# The Teacher After the Tutor

**Status:** Long-form editorial manuscript | **Theme:** Education and the next generation of expertise | **Candidate tags:** education, sociology-of-science, ethics

Imagine a classroom in which every student has access to an excellent tutor. The tutor can explain a difficult concept in several ways. It can recognize a mistake, offer an example, and give each student the amount of practice they need. A student who has fallen behind can receive assistance without waiting for the teacher to finish helping someone else.

I hope that such systems become available to children. I also think the capability to provide this kind of instruction will become increasingly difficult for a human teacher to compete with. If we can establish what a student needs to learn, and have a reliable way to investigate whether the student has learned it, much of the work of improving instruction can enter an automated process.

What, then, is the purpose of having a teacher?

I do not think the answer will be found by identifying a way in which teachers can continue to explain things better than machines. I expect the distinction between teaching as a means of delivering knowledge and the teacher as a participant in an educational institution to become much more visible.

## It is possible for the tutor to win

We already have some useful evidence concerning the first part of the question. In a [2025 study by Greg Kestin and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12179260/), students in an undergraduate physics course encountered two lessons through different arrangements. They experienced an AI-supported lesson and an active-learning classroom lesson, with the conditions exchanged between groups. The study enrolled 194 eligible students. In the evaluated lessons, the researchers reported higher immediate post-test performance for the AI tutor condition and less time spent learning.

This is a meaningful result. It concerns a particular tutor, a particular set of lessons, and a particular population. It does not establish the superiority of every AI tutor, and it provides little direct evidence about a child's experience over several years of school.

Still, I would not try to preserve the role of a teacher by denying the possibility that machines will become better at many forms of instruction. A system can provide an explanation at a moment when a learner needs it, examine the learner's response, and use that information to choose the next activity. An increasingly capable system can make that process more effective.

The result may change the organization of schools. A teacher could spend less time delivering the same explanation to a group and more time understanding what is happening among the students. They might use the tutoring system to help decide when to introduce an activity, when a student needs different guidance, and which things the class could investigate together.

That is one possible organization of education after tutoring becomes abundant. Other organizations are possible. A school could use the system to reduce the number of teachers and leave a smaller group of adults responsible for supervision. It could also move much of the students' activity outside the classroom. The improvement in instruction does not tell us which of these arrangements the school should choose.

## What happens over the course of a school day?

Consider a child in a classroom with other children. They need to learn how to perform some mathematical operations. They also encounter someone who approaches a problem very differently from the way they do. They have opportunities to explain something to a friend, participate in an unfamiliar activity, and work with a group on a problem that none of them knows how to solve.

There are difficult moments as well. A child is excluded from a group. Two students disagree about something they have made together. Someone refuses to participate in an activity the others regard as important. These situations are part of the education of the children, even when they have little to do with the knowledge an examination will ask them to demonstrate.

The teacher's role can involve participating in those situations and deciding how to respond to them. A response can establish what the children are expected to do for one another, how they can express a disagreement, and when the group needs to change its practice. The teacher is also part of a larger arrangement involving families, other teachers, and the school.

Gert Biesta gives a useful account of the different purposes of education in [Good Education in an Age of Measurement](https://www.stir.ac.uk/research/hub/publication/892564). He distinguishes the development of knowledge and skills, participation in existing social and cultural arrangements, and the development of the learner as a subject. His argument helps us ask what a school should be doing rather than treating the measurement of outcomes as sufficient to decide its purposes.

I am interested in the consequences of that distinction when the technical work of tutoring becomes easier. If a school can provide students with excellent instruction through an AI system, it acquires more freedom to decide what students should do together and what kinds of relationships should organize their education.

Those decisions deserve to remain visible. Improving the amount of knowledge children acquire is a valuable achievement. It does not by itself determine the kind of community in which we want them to acquire that knowledge.

## A teacher is someone to whom a child can return

Suppose a child has been having difficulty participating in class. An intelligent system may be able to recognize a pattern in the child's behavior and suggest an appropriate activity. A teacher may also recognize the pattern. Both could help the child in many circumstances.

There is another question about the relationship. The child may have encountered the teacher through many activities. They may understand why the teacher makes certain decisions, know what they can expect when they ask for help, and have opportunities to explain a problem that the teacher has misunderstood. The teacher can take responsibility for a judgment and reconsider it in light of what the child says or does.

Machines may become capable of supporting relationships with many of these characteristics. I would be careful about claiming that a particular conversational or perceptual ability will remain unavailable to them. The institutional question concerns what kinds of relationships people will choose to establish around the education of children.

My bet is that many societies will continue to place a human adult at the center of the sustained educational relationship in primary and secondary schools. I expect children to have teachers who know them through a range of experiences and who participate in decisions about their education. The adult's role may become quite different from the one we currently associate with delivering a lesson.

There are some reasons to expect this arrangement. Parents and schools may want someone within their community to take responsibility for how a child is treated. They may want an adult who can participate in the school's social life and explain a difficult decision in relation to that child's circumstances. A teacher may also help students engage with people and practices outside the conditions selected by a personalized tutor.

These reasons concern the institution of education. They do not follow automatically from a benchmark comparing teaching performance.

## A society has to choose this arrangement

The prediction is not guaranteed. It costs money to maintain a school in which teachers can establish meaningful relationships with their students. A school facing serious staffing difficulties may have a strong reason to reduce the amount of time students spend with a human teacher. Families may develop positive experiences with automated learning arrangements and come to prefer them.

The result could also be unequal. People with more resources might purchase an educational experience organized around sustained relationships with adults, while other students spend most of their time interacting with a system. The presence of a human supervisor would not necessarily give those students the kind of relationship I am describing.

We should therefore examine what happens in schools rather than simply count whether a position called teacher continues to exist. How much time can a teacher actually spend with the children for whom they are responsible? Can they make meaningful educational decisions? Do they organize activities through which children learn to participate in a shared world? Are students able to return to that person when something about their experience needs to be understood?

If the answers become largely negative, the educational bet will have failed in an important sense. A teacher whose name remains attached to a group of students but who seldom has an opportunity to know them is a different kind of institution from the one I expect.

I am fairly optimistic about the capabilities of future tutors. I am less certain about how societies will distribute the opportunities those capabilities create. That is precisely why I think the social organization of education deserves more attention.

A machine may eventually teach a child nearly any subject with extraordinary effectiveness. The school will still embody a decision about what the child is there to experience, whom they are expected to encounter, and who will share responsibility for that part of their life. I expect the future of education to be decided increasingly through those questions.
