# The Household Is a Moving Target

**Status:** editorial draft / argument development | **Theme:** Embodied intelligence | **Candidate tags:** robotics, participatory-design, ai

A household changes even when its furniture stays in the same place. People return from school, guests arrive, a child grows older, and a task that was welcome yesterday becomes intrusive today. A robot that learns a home's distribution may be learning a moving social arrangement.

## Working argument

In a conventional generalization problem, a new environment differs from the environments used for training. Household robotics must handle this problem, but there are also changes that are created by the people and the robot living together. A person may change where an object belongs after the robot begins tidying it. Different residents may have competing expectations. A guest may be welcome to do something a machine is not.

The distinction matters because improved prediction of a past routine may not reveal what ought to happen next. More observations can help the system notice that a resident's behavior changed. They cannot by themselves authorize a decision when two residents want incompatible arrangements. A home requires mechanisms for determining whose instructions apply, when they expire, and how people can challenge a persistent rule.

I expect some of the difficult questions of domestic robotics to arise from this continual reorganization of practice. A system with robust physical generalization could make these questions visible on a scale that fragile robots have not permitted. The interesting research setting is the developing relationship between capability and local authority, rather than a static test house with increasingly many objects.

## Challenge

Do not use a social example to deny the value of technical generalization. Models can adapt to changing routines and infer many preferences. The empirical question is where adaptation suffices and where people need an explicit way to negotiate the shared environment.
