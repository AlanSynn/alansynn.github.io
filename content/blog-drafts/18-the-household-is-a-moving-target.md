# The Household Is a Moving Target

**Status:** editorial draft / argument development | **Theme:** Embodied intelligence | **Candidate tags:** robotics, participatory-design, ai

A household is an environment whose rules change as people live in it. A robot might learn where everything belongs and still encounter a disagreement about where something should go today. The difficulty lies partly in the changing social arrangement through which the objects acquire their purposes.

Consider a household with a shared table. On most days the residents want the table to be clear. On one afternoon a child has left an unfinished project on it. Moving the pieces into their usual storage positions might make the robot's activity look successful according to its earlier experience. It could also destroy an arrangement that the child needs to preserve.

A robot that can recognize a new arrangement of objects has acquired an important technical capability. It may also learn from the residents' behavior that the usual rule has an exception. But some changes concern the authority to determine what should happen. One resident wants the project left alone; another needs space for dinner. Knowing each person's preference does not automatically establish whose instruction should govern the next action.

The conflict could recur even if the robot knew every resident's preferences perfectly. The table is a shared resource, and two legitimate activities may need it at the same time. A system can help arrange a temporary exception or ask for a decision. The authority to establish the rule belongs to the household's arrangement rather than the accuracy of a preference prediction.

This is one reason to distinguish generalization to a new physical environment from adaptation to a shared social practice. Stronger models can learn a great deal from repeated use, and many changes in a household will be easy to accommodate. A useful arrangement may also need ways for people to specify which activities have priority, how long an instruction applies, and how another resident can challenge it.

The rules need not all become elaborate procedures. Many ordinary tasks have satisfactory defaults. An explicit negotiation may be useful only when an unfamiliar situation creates a consequential conflict. The design question concerns how the system recognizes the difference and makes its assumptions intelligible.

Domestic robotics could become particularly interesting after many physical capabilities are dependable enough to be used regularly. We would be able to investigate how the robot participates in changing practices rather than repeatedly evaluate it against a fixed description of the home. A successful robot may need to learn the household many times, because the people who inhabit it will continue to make it a different place.
